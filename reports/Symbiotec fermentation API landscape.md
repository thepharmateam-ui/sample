# Symbiotec's steroid franchise sits beside classical fermentation

Frost & Sullivan (F&S), in Concord Biotech's July-2023 RHP, defines fermentation-derived APIs as ingredients made by microbes or cell lines, either directly or semi-synthetically, and sizes a small-molecule subset that this report treats as classical fermentation [S1 p.109]. Our grouping puts these molecules in four areas (based on [S1 pp.111, 151–152; S4 p.1]). Immunosuppressants include tacrolimus, cyclosporine, sirolimus and the mycophenolates [S1 p.152]. Oncology covers rapalogs, mitomycin and anthracyclines [S1 pp.115, 151–152]. Anti-infectives span mupirocin, glycopeptides, polymyxins, echinocandins [S1 p.152] and beta-lactam starting materials [S4 p.1]. The fourth area is statins [S1 p.152]. That market was worth about **$11–12bn in 2022** (USD 11bn in the RHP text, USD 12bn in its chart [S1 pp.109–110]), inside a **$219bn global API market** [S1 p.106]. Symbiotec Pharmalab listed on 1-Sep-2026 [S5] after an RHP dated 18-Aug-2026 [S3 cover page]. Our view: at its core, it is not a classical fermenter. Its core business uses microbes to convert soy phytosterols into steroid building blocks (androstenedione, 9-OH-AD and bisnor alcohol), followed by many steps of chemistry [S3 pp.220, 240]. F&S's own 2026 taxonomy files that route under **"bioconversion", not "classical fermentation"** [S3 p.187]. The markets Symbiotec actually serves are small and slow-growing: **$421.2m in corticosteroid APIs and $300.7m in steroid-hormone APIs in 2025**, each growing 1–3% a year [S3 pp.191–192]. Its true classical-fermentation market is only **$491.9m** [S3 p.187]. The headline "fermentation API" market of $100.4bn [S3 p.188] is 82% biologics (our calculation: $82.3bn biologics ÷ $100.4bn [S3 pp.188, 200]). Our view: its competitors fall into two groups that barely overlap. Our view: Concord and its immunosuppressant-led global rivals [S1 pp.121–122] matter to Symbiotec only through its small classical-fermentation line. Symbiotec's website names that line as mupirocin, teicoplanin and serratiopeptidase [S6; S2 p.240; S3 p.235 names only mupirocin and serratiopeptidase], and the first two are Concord products [S1 pp.151–152]. In its core products, Symbiotec competes with Chinese and Western steroid makers such as Zhejiang Xianju, Tianjin Tianyao/Tianfa, Pfizer CentreOne, Bayer and Curia [S3 pp.203–205]. Every industry figure in both prospectuses comes from F&S reports paid for by the issuer [S1 p.6; S2 p.7; S3 p.7], and the baselines shift from one edition to the next. Global API rises from $219bn (2022) [S1 p.106] to $305.5bn (2025) [S3 p.184]. The biologic share of API swings from 37% [S1 p.106] to 24% [S2 p.187] to 35.7% [S3 p.184]. Symbiotec's claimed volume share in steroid hormones fell from **44.2% in the DRHP** (FY25 [S2 p.29]) **to 23.8% in the RHP** (FY26, excluding androstenedione and hydroxyprogesterone caproate [S3 p.218]). Our view: analysts should anchor on one edition, the Symbiotec RHP's CY2025 series, and treat comparisons across documents as directional only.

## Eight therapy families make up the classical fermentation universe

The Concord RHP sets the broad definition that this report uses; the 2026 F&S report in the Symbiotec RHP draws narrower lines [S3 pp.185, 187]. Per the RHP, "fermentation-derived APIs are active ingredients or intermediates made using microbial or cell line host fermentation", and they "can be naturally derived as well as semi-synthetically processed with a combination of fermentation and chemical synthesis" [S1 p.109]. The RHP says they span "vaccines to anti-cancer cytotoxic drugs, antibiotics and antifungals, hormonal products, immunosuppressants, and vitamins" [S1 p.109]. It defines semi-synthesis as microbial fermentation and purification to an intermediate, followed by "chemical synthesis to obtain final products" [S1 p.119]. F&S then sizes a narrower **"small molecule fermentation API" market** [S1 p.109] and splits it into only three therapy areas: immunosuppressants, oncology and anti-infectives [S1 p.111]. Concord's business section sorts its **23 APIs** [S1 p.141] into fermentation-based products and semi-synthetics "manufactured with our in-house fermentation-based key starting materials" [S1 pp.151–152]. We infer that "classical fermentation" in the Concord RHP's sense, which this report follows, means fermentation-derived small molecules, natural or semi-synthetic, and excludes recombinant biologics (based on [S1 pp.109, 151–152]); the Concord RHP itself does not use the phrase "classical fermentation".

Other sources extend the list. India's bulk-drug PLI scheme puts **penicillin G, 7-ACA, erythromycin thiocyanate and clavulanic acid** in Target Segment I, "Key Fermentation Based KSMs" [S4 p.1; S8]. It puts **rifampicin, betamethasone, dexamethasone and prednisolone** in Target Segment II, "Fermentation Based Niche" products, and Symbiotec appears among the TS-II approved applicants [S4 p.1]. The 2026 F&S report in the Symbiotec RHP draws "classical fermentation" more narrowly: "utilization of naturally occurring microbes… to cultivate and produce natural metabolites", applied to "antibiotics (e.g., penicillin), amino acids, vitamins, and organic acids" [S3 p.187]. Symbiotec's own pipeline under that heading covers "anti-infectives, anti-fungals, immunosuppressants, enzymes and anti-parasitics" [S3 p.220]. Its website and DRHP name three launched classical fermentation APIs, **mupirocin, teicoplanin and serratiopeptidase** [S6; S2 p.240], while the RHP drops teicoplanin [S3 p.235] (covered in its own section below). Vitamin D3, algal DHA and a generic Premarin API sit alongside them as planned speciality launches [S3 pp.231, 235; S7; S9 p.5]. Concord has since launched **nystatin** (FY25; already in its 2023 RHP portfolio, without regulatory filings [S1 p.151]) and **fusidic acid** (FY26) as second-source anti-infectives [S10 pp.3, 7].

## Symbiotec's steroid route starts with bioconversion and ends in chemistry

Symbiotec's RHP describes its process plainly [S3 p.240]. "The initial stages of API manufacturing involve bioconversion of phytosterol to the desired starting materials or building blocks like **bisnor alcohol, 9-hydroxy androstenedione or androstenedione**" [S3 p.240]. These building blocks then go through acetylation, Birch reduction, bromination, cyanation, epoxidation, fluorination, Grignard and hydrogenation steps [S3 p.240]. In one worked example, a single key starting material made from 9-OH-AD feeds a chain of **four chemically derived APIs** [S3 p.240]. The phytosterols are soybean-oil by-products [S9 p.4; S3 p.220], partly imported from the US [S3 p.232]. They are "converted into KSMs through bio-transformation processes using fermentation" [S3 p.232]. The RHP does not name the microbes [S3, full-text search]. In the literature, *Mycobacterium* side-chain cleavage of phytosterols to AD is a commercial route dating to the mid-1970s [S21]. A *Mycobacterium* strain already used in industry reaches about 31 g/L of 4-AD at pilot scale [S22]. Fungal and bacterial hydroxylation and dehydrogenation steps carry intermediates toward hydrocortisone and prednisolone [S18; S19].

Our view: the distinction from classical fermentation is mechanistic. In classical fermentation, the microbe builds the drug's carbon skeleton from cheap sugars as a natural secondary metabolite; tacrolimus and mupirocin are examples. In steroid biotransformation, the four-ring skeleton comes ready-made from plants, and the microbe only trims and modifies it. (Based on F&S's definitions of classical fermentation as microbes that "produce natural metabolites" and of bioconversion as converting a supplied substrate [S3 p.187]; F&S's list of key fermentation nutrients, "corn syrup, molasses, amino acids" [S3 p.213]; Concord's classification of tacrolimus and mupirocin as fermentation-based [S1 p.152]; phytosterol described as "a plant-based steroid" [S9 p.4]; and microbial side-chain removal and 11β-hydroxylation of steroids [S21; S18].) **F&S's 2026 taxonomy makes exactly this split.** It defines "Bioconversion (Biotransformation)" as "utilization of microbial enzymes to selectively convert a substrate into a more valuable compound", with "steroid modification" as the first listed application [S3 p.187]. Classical fermentation sits in a separate row [S3 p.187].

Our view: the boundary is not applied consistently, though. The same 2026 report tags eight of the top ten global corticosteroid and steroid-hormone APIs by volume, all of which Symbiotec supplies, as using fermentation ("Yes") and two as "Yes – indirectly" [S3 pp.204, 206]. It calls "antibiotics and steroids" the fermentation API market's "traditional stronghold" [S3 p.190]. Concord's 2023 F&S report counts "hormonal products" as fermentation-derived [S1 p.109], and the PLI scheme calls corticosteroids "fermentation based niche" APIs [S4]. Our view: by Concord's own definition of semi-synthesis (a fermented intermediate plus chemistry to the final product [S1 pp.109, 152]), Symbiotec's steroids look structurally like Concord's mycophenolate mofetil, which Concord makes from in-house fermentation-based key starting materials [S1 p.152]. Our view: the difference is weighting. Concord ferments the complex part of the molecule, while Symbiotec buys the complex part from soybeans and adds most of the value through chemistry (based on [S1 p.152; S9 p.4; S3 p.240]).

Our view: capacity data confirms that weighting. Symbiotec has **584.67 MT of chemical-synthesis capacity** [S3 p.218] but only **300 KL of pharma fermentation** at Pithampur (4 × 5 KL plus 8 × 35 KL [S3 p.221], 72.91% used in FY26 [S3 pp.52, 244]). The other 400 KL of the headline 700 KL sits at Ujjain [S3 pp.218–219, 241]. That plant is FSSAI- and FSSC-approved nutraceutical-grade capacity [S3 pp.30, 222] built for industrial-biotech and non-pharmaceutical CDMO [S3 pp.29, 236, 241]; we infer it is not meant for steroid APIs. Concord's 1,250 KL, by contrast, is all pharma fermentation [S1 p.142]. Hawkwood, a partner that calls itself "100% in with Symbiotec", promotes the CDMO side on cost (about 50% below Western competition) and build speed (400 KL in 18 months) [S23]. Our view: its view is advocacy rather than independent diligence (based on [S23]).

Our view: Symbiotec's true classical-fermentation business is new and still immaterial. The company launched "classical fermentation-based APIs at the Pithampur Facility" in 2023 [S3 p.266] and holds a **ten-year take-or-pay contract** for them [S3 pp.233, 236]. Even so, APIs, which are overwhelmingly steroids [S9 p.4], made up **96.07% of FY26 revenue**, and CDMO made up 0.13% [S3 pp.28–29]. Our view: the fermentation step's main strategic value is security of key starting materials (KSMs) (based on [S3 p.232]). Symbiotec claims make-versus-buy flexibility on more than 80% of products by revenue [S3 p.220]. F&S notes "China's Cortisone and steroid export dominance" [S3 p.212]. Yet raw-material imports from China were **52.8%, 16.8% and 23.9% of total expenses in FY24, FY25 and FY26** [S3 pp.36–37]. ICICI Direct says the company still buys 45–50% of its requirements from China despite "90–95% backward integration" [S9 p.4]. For comparison, Concord's China imports were 32.0% of materials consumed in FY23 [S1 p.28].

**Our view: for policy and for headline market sizing, Symbiotec belongs in the fermentation-derived API universe. In technology and economics, it is a steroid chemistry company with an in-house biotransformation front end**, plus a small classical and precision-fermentation business (based on [S4; S3 pp.28–29, 187, 202, 240]). Our view: Concord is therefore an imperfect comparable. Our view: the 8-percentage-point EBITDA margin gap between them in FY26 (26.6% versus 34.8% [S3 pp.155, 217]; our calculation: 34.8% − 26.6% = 8.2 points) fits that difference, though it does not prove it.

## Symbiotec's classical fermentation line names three molecules, two of them Concord's

Symbiotec's website lists its classical fermentation products apart from its steroid catalogue [S6; S24]. The homepage "What's New" panel, under the heading "Classical Fermentation-Based APIs", says: "We have already launched Fermentation-Based APIs such as Mupirocin, Teicoplanin and Serratiopeptidase, among others, and we intend to build on this foundation by introducing additional High-Value, low-volume APIs in niche therapeutic areas." [S6]. The same panel lists speciality ingredients (vegan vitamin D3 and algal DHA) and CDMO services for alternative proteins and enzymes [S6]. The downloadable product list (PDF created 31-Jan-2026) covers only corticosteroid and steroid-hormone APIs (it headlines "over 60" such APIs and lists those with US DMFs and CEPs), and no fermentation product appears in it [S24]. None of the three classical fermentation APIs therefore shows a US DMF or CEP on the company's own regulatory list [S24; S25].

The offer documents carry the same sentence with one change [S2 p.240; S3 p.235]. The DRHP matches the website word for word: "Mupirocin, Teicoplanin and Serratiopeptidase, among others" [S2 p.240; S6]. The RHP, eight months later (our calculation: DRHP dated 19-Dec-2025, RHP dated 18-Aug-2026 [S2, cover; S3, cover]), reads "Mupirocin and Serratiopeptidase, among others", so **teicoplanin has been dropped** [S3 p.235]. Neither document explains the change (teicoplanin appears nowhere else in either filing [S2; S3, full-text search]). Both describe the pipeline as anti-infectives, anti-fungals, immunosuppressants, enzymes and anti-parasitics [S2 p.224; S3 p.220], and both tie the business to the 2023 Pithampur launch [S2 p.270; S3 p.266] and the ten-year take-or-pay contract [S2 p.238; S3 p.233]. F&S footnotes its $491.9m "classical fermentation API market" as including "products from Symbiotec's product portfolio" [S3 p.187]; we infer these molecules are its likely components, although F&S never lists them [S3 pp.169–217, full-text search].

## A $305bn API market narrows to a $722m steroid pool

The global API market depends on which F&S edition you use [S1 p.106; S27 p.42; S2 p.186; S3 p.184]. In the Concord RHP it is **$219bn in 2022, rising to $278bn by 2026 (6.1% CAGR)** [S1 p.106]. Biologics are 37% of that, and captive production is about 60% versus 40% merchant [S1 p.106]. The therapy areas it breaks out are oncology at $42bn (the largest), anti-infectives at $32bn and immunosuppressants at $16bn [S1 p.107]. Anthem's F&S report (Dec-2024) puts it at $262.2bn in 2023 [S27 cover, p.42]. The Symbiotec DRHP gives $285.4bn for 2024, rising to $399.9bn by 2029 [S2 p.186]. The Symbiotec RHP gives **$305.5bn in 2025, rising to $424.6bn by 2030 (6.8%)**, with small molecules at 64.3% and North America at 53.4% of value [S3 p.184].

The two fermentation figures measure different things [S1 p.109; S3 p.188]. Concord's F&S sizes only small molecules: **$11bn in its text [S1 p.109] or $12bn in its chart [S1 p.110] for 2022, rising to about $14bn by 2026 at 3.6%** [S1 pp.109–110]. In volume terms that is 51,519 MT rising to 61,673 MT (4.6%) [S1 pp.109–110]. North America takes 43% of value on 13% of volume, while Asia takes 51% of volume but only about 25% of value [S1 p.110]. Symbiotec's F&S sizes all modalities: **$100.4bn in 2025, rising to $162.5bn by 2030 (10.1%)**, of which biologics are $82.3bn [S3 p.188]. Small molecules are $18.1bn in the chart (5.5% CAGR) [S3 p.188], and the "classical fermentation API market" is just **$491.9m, growing 1.5–4.0%** [S3 p.187]. A footnote says that market "includes products from Symbiotec's product portfolio" [S3 p.187]. Our view: on their starting points the two editions agree more than they seem to. Small-molecule fermentation was about $11–12bn around 2020–22 in both [S1 pp.109–110] (the Symbiotec RHP shows $12.0bn for 2020 [S3 p.188]), and in both it is **about 5–6% of global API value** (our calculation: $11–12bn ÷ $219bn = 5.0–5.5% for 2022 [S1 pp.106, 109–110]; $18.1–19.1bn ÷ $305.5bn = 5.9–6.3% for 2025 [S3 pp.184, 188, 200]). They split on growth [S1 p.109; S3 p.188]. Concord's forecast path implies about $13.3bn by 2025 (our calculation: the $12bn chart value for 2022 [S1 p.110] grown at the 3.6% forecast CAGR [S1 p.109] for three years), while Symbiotec's F&S reports $18.1–19.1bn [S3 pp.188, 200]. Neither document explains the gap [S1 pp.109–110; S3 pp.187–188, 200].

India's API market is only partly sized [S1 p.122; S3 pp.207–208]. The Concord RHP puts it at **$17bn in 2022**: $12bn consumed domestically (forecast at $19bn by 2026, 11.1% CAGR) and $5bn exported [S1 pp.122–123]. On RHP numbers that implies about $15.9bn (our calculation: 5.2% × $305.5bn [S3 pp.184, 208]), close to Concord's $17bn for 2022 [S1 p.122]. Third-party estimates range from $11.6bn for FY25 (Ken Research) [S30] to $14.18bn for 2025 (Mordor) [S31] and $19.21bn for 2025 (IMARC) [S32]. **No document sizes an Indian fermentation API market** [S1 pp.102–138; S2 pp.210–211; S3 pp.207–208]. Our view: the steroid-hormone share revision (row 7) needs the closest look. The RHP excludes androstenedione and hydroxyprogesterone caproate from the hormone basket [S3 pp.205, 218]; we infer that this change of definition mechanically lowers the share. Progesterone, the largest hormone product [S3 p.206], also fell from 26.1% to 21.2% [S2 p.209; S3 p.206], so we infer that part of the drop is a real loss of share. An outside review (Listings Tracker) urges caution on the RHP's 38.2% and 23.8% shares because the market is defined around Symbiotec's focus products, but it does not compare them with the DRHP figures [S49]. The corticosteroid share edged up from 36.2% to 38.2% [S2 p.208; S3 p.218], even though Symbiotec's prednisolone share fell from 13.0% to 4.9% [S2 p.209; S3 p.206].

Company figures also differ between sources. The RHP's business section claims "over 60" APIs [S3 p.219], while its F&S section counts 44 active APIs [S3 p.202]. The RHP lists 43 US DMFs and 23 CEPs [S3 pp.202, 219], while the desk note gives 44 and 25 [S7]. FY26 EBITDA is ₹2,319.7m (26.59%) in the RHP [S3 p.151] but "Rs 2.4bn (27.4%)" in the desk note [S7]. We infer that the gap is definitional. The RHP's EBITDA includes other income (₹31.1m), is struck after a ₹89.9m exceptional item, and its margin is on total income [S3 pp.86, 151]. Stripping out both items gives about ₹2,379m, or 27.4% of revenue (our calculation: ₹2,319.7m + ₹89.9m − ₹31.1m, ÷ ₹8,691.5m [S3 pp.86, 151]). Ventura's series likewise keeps other income, bill-discounting charges and the exceptional item outside EBITDA [S50 p.4]. Within the Concord RHP, export growth is 7–9% in the text [S1 p.122] but 6.6% in the chart [S1 p.124]. Its $5bn export figure for 2022 [S1 pp.122–123] is also about 12% above what the Symbiotec RHP's own 2020–25 export path implies for that year (our calculation: $5bn ÷ ~$4.46bn, interpolating $4.2bn in 2020 to $4.9bn in 2025 at 3.1% a year [S3 p.208]).

## Conclusion

Our view: the "fermentation" label on Symbiotec's equity story points to markets its core business does not address (based on [S3 pp.187–188, 191–192]). The steroid pool it serves grows 1–3% a year [S3 pp.191–192]. Symbiotec already holds about 13% of that pool by value (our calculation: FY26 API revenue ₹8,350m [S3 p.155] at an assumed ₹88/$ ≈ $95m, ÷ $722m [S3 pp.191–192]; about 12% at the RHP's 31-Mar-2026 rate of ₹94.65/$ [S3 p.24]), and 76–80% of volume in hydrocortisone, testosterone and methylprednisolone [S3 p.205]. Its own classical-fermentation market is under $0.5bn and growing about as slowly [S3 p.187]. The double-digit growth rates in its F&S report belong to biologics and CDMO [S3 pp.188, 200, 214], which brought in 0.13% of FY26 revenue [S3 p.29]. Q1FY27 consolidated profit then fell by about half, down 53% year on year [S48]. We infer that start-up costs at the new businesses drove the fall, because API EBITDA rose 9% in the quarter and the desk note flags new-business opex [S7]. Our view: growth in the core steroid business therefore has to come from mix, pricing, KSM costs and new molecules such as conjugated estrogens [S3 p.235], not from the market. Our view: in effect, investors are buying a CDMO and injectables option funded by a mature steroid cash engine whose growth depends on taking share. Our view: a steroid market of under $1bn ($722m per F&S [S3 pp.191–192]; ~$1bn per management [S9 p.4]) caps that cash engine more tightly than its "fermentation API" positioning suggests.

For comparables, Concord matches Symbiotec on API revenue [S3 p.155] but, in our view, not on market. The two share only mupirocin and, per the website and DRHP, teicoplanin [S1 pp.141, 151–152; S6; S2 p.240; S3 p.235]. Our view: Symbiotec's pricing power is set by Xianju, Tianyao/Tianfa, Henan Lihua and the Western legacy producers, not by Indian fermenters (the rivals are named in [S3 pp.203–205]). Several of those rivals are shrinking [S43; S41; S42], which we infer helps Symbiotec gain share. No source ties the decline to steroids: EUROAPI attributes its 2025 sales fall mainly to non-steroid factors [S43], and the Chinese companies' income statements give no reason [S41; S42]. Our view: a sensible comp set mixes Chinese and European steroid makers for competitive economics with Concord and Laurus for valuing the fermentation platform (based on [S3 pp.203–205, 216]). Our view: three questions should go to management. Which molecules make up the $491.9m classical-fermentation market [S3 p.187], and why did teicoplanin drop out of the RHP's list of launched products [S2 p.240; S3 p.235]? What are the company's value shares, as opposed to volume shares [S3 pp.205–207]? What exactly explains the hormone share falling from 44.2% to 23.8% [S2 p.208; S3 p.218]? Our view: until these are answered, the F&S share claims are best treated as company supply data scaled to a market that F&S defined to fit the company (based on [S3 pp.191, 205]).
