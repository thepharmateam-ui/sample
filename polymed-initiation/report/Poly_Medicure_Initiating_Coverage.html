Polymed in 10 points

  1. What it is. Polymed makes single-use medical devices: IV cannulas and infusion sets, dialysis consumables and machines, blood bags and collection tubes, catheters for urology, surgery, anaesthesia and critical care, and since FY26 cardiology catheters and stents and trauma implants. It runs 15 plants in 5 countries and sells in 125+ countries. It is the world's #3 maker of IV cannulas by its own account.
  2. How it makes money. It sells very large volumes of low-priced devices, with Indian price points of Rs. 10-20 for mass products. Low labour cost, automation, in-house tools and machines, and vertical integration let it earn a standalone gross margin of 71.5% and an operating EBITDA margin of 26.8% in FY26 (Section A2).
  3. Infusion is half of revenue, and falling as a share. Infusion was 53% of FY26 revenue, down from 68% in FY20. The statutory BRSR shows what sits behind the "others" bucket for the standalone company:
    • blood management and collection, 9.5% of standalone turnover;
    • surgery and wound drainage, 5.3%;
    • urology, 3.0%;
    • prefilled flush syringes, 10.2%, one of the largest single product families.
  4. Two-thirds of revenue is exported. 69% of FY26 revenue came from outside India. Europe and the rest of the world each account for about a third, and India for the balance. The US is still small at $3.5-4mn a year.
  5. FY26 was a reset. Standalone revenue grew only 4% against consolidated guidance of 20%. Management cited destocking by European distributors, Chinese product diverted from the US to Europe, faster Suez transit and late MDR renewals. 1QFY27 re-accelerated to 12.4% organic growth (Section A3).
  6. Renal is the second engine, and the most contested. Polymed is India's first domestic dialyser maker. It had about 1,000 dialysis machines installed by FY26. Duty-free Chinese dialysers pushed renal revenue down 3.8% yoy in 1QFY27. The company's anti-dumping application claims an injury margin of 20% (Section A4.3).
  7. Acquisitions add cardiology and orthopaedics, with a weak start. PendraCare (Netherlands) and Citieffe (Italy) contributed Rs. 723mn of revenue in 1QFY27. PendraCare lost Rs. 141mn before tax in its first six months under Polymed; Citieffe was near breakeven. The in-house stent programme had about 13,000 implants by Jul-2026 (Sections A4.8, A4.9).
  8. Margin drivers are mostly company-specific.
    • Pressures: raw materials (32.4% of standalone revenue), a 35% increase in Haryana's minimum wage from Apr-2026, freight, and dilution from the acquired businesses.
    • Offsets: automation (530 assembly machines and 130 robots), mix and a weaker rupee.
    • See Sections A2 and A7.
  9. The industry backdrop is mixed.
    • Tailwinds: India's device policy (PLI, NMDP and procurement preference), EU MDR attrition among small suppliers, and US duties on Chinese syringes and needles.
    • Risks: a possible cap on trade margins in India, Chinese pricing in Europe and in renal, and US tariff volatility.
    • See Part B.
  10. Capital allocation and governance deserve diligence.
    • Unspent QIP money: Rs. 4.8bn of the Aug-2024 QIP was unused at 31-Mar-2026.
    • Job work with a promoter firm: 84% of job work goes to promoter partnership Vitromed.
    • A reclassified promoter group: two LLPs once counted as public shareholders were moved into the promoter group.
    • High executive pay: the MD earned 497x the median employee.
    • Offsetting strengths: clean audits, a CRISIL AA-/Positive rating and net cash of ~Rs. 5.0bn (Section A9).

Part A – The business

A1 What Polymed sells and where the revenue comes from

Poly Medicure makes single-use medical devices: the cannulas, infusion sets, fistula needles, bloodlines, blood bags, catheters, tubes and drains that a hospital consumes in very large volumes and throws away after one patient.

  • Scale. It sells 225+ products across 13 specialities, from 15 plants in 5 countries with ~1.9bn units of annual capacity. The products reach 125+ countries through 1,000+ distributors.
  • Anchor product. The IV cannula: Polymed describes itself as the third-largest maker globally, and the MD put its global share at almost 11% on ~600mn units a year.
  • Unit economics. Most devices sell for a few rupees: management cites price points of Rs. 10-20 per infusion device. It sold 320mn units devices in 3QFY25 alone.
  • Scale of sales. FY26 consolidated revenue was Rs. 18.8bn.

The business model in one line: a very high-volume, low-price consumables business, built on in-house automation, that has earned group operating EBITDA margins in the mid-to-high twenties (Section A2). It is now adding higher-priced devices in cardiology, orthopaedics and oncology, where management says a single unit sells for thousands of rupees, sometimes above Rs. 20,000.

Five therapy lines are disclosed, two of them new

Polymed reports revenue in five buckets:

  1. Infusion therapy: IV cannulas, infusion sets and accessories.
  2. Renal: dialysis consumables and machines.
  3. Cardiology: reported separately since the PendraCare acquisition.
  4. Orthopaedics: Citieffe, reported separately since its acquisition.
  5. Others.

"Others" pools every other speciality: blood management, anaesthesia and respiratory, critical care, diagnostics, urology, gastroenterology, surgery and wound drainage, and the vascular-access and oncology products of Plan1Health. On calls, management has declined to break it out further. The MD said on the 4QFY26 call that the new lines are 'very new businesses' and 'we don't call out those numbers specifically because confidentially, we don't want to share what company is doing'.

The company's statutory sustainability report (BRSR) nonetheless gives a finer split of standalone turnover, which we use below and in Section A4. It does not estimate anything beyond what that report prints.

The mix shift has been fast:

  • FY20 to FY26: infusion's share fell from 68% to 53%.
  • 4QFY25 to 4QFY26: management put infusion at 50% of revenue in 4QFY26 against 57% a year earlier.
  • 1QFY27, the first quarter with a full five-line split:
    • infusion 50%
    • others 27%
    • orthopaedics 9%
    • renal 8%
    • cardiology 5%

Management frames this as de-risking from what it calls 'overdependence' on infusion. The shift also moves revenue toward lines with higher gross margins but, for now, lower EBITDA margins (Section A2).

The sustainability report gives the only finer product split

Every listed Indian company must list, in its Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report, its main products with each one's share of turnover. Polymed's BRSR does this for the standalone company, which excludes the foreign subsidiaries, on a product classification broader than the one in its investor presentations.

It gives the following for FY26:

  • Blood management and blood collection: 9.5% of standalone turnover, or about Rs. 1.6bn (our calculation). That makes it the third-largest line after infusion and dialysis.
  • Surgery and wound drainage: 5.3%.
  • Urology: 3.0%.
  • Anaesthesia and respiratory: a separate line at 2.9% in FY25. It has no separate line in FY26, which suggests it was reclassified.

A separate BRSR table on product life-cycle assessment shows that prefilled saline flush syringes (3 mL, 5 mL and 10 mL) alone were 10.2% of FY26 standalone turnover, about Rs. 1.7bn (our calculation). That makes flush syringes one of the largest single product families after IV cannulas.

Two-thirds of revenue is earned outside India

Polymed has always been an exporter: 73% of FY20 revenue came from outside India. International revenue was still 69% of FY26 revenue (and 72% in 1QFY27, lifted by the acquisitions).

  • Domestic: India has been the fastest-growing region, with a company-stated FY20-FY26 CAGR of 21%. Its share rose from 27% to 31%.
  • Europe: a CAGR of 20%, lifting its share from 29% to 32%.
  • RoW: Africa, West Asia, Latin America and the rest of Asia grew at 15%, and their share fell from 46% to 37%.

The MD's stated long-run aim is a mix of 1/3 India and 2/3 RoW, roughly today's balance.

What the disclosure does and does not tell us

Company disclosure is good on:

  • consolidated and standalone P&L by quarter;
  • domestic versus international revenue, with Europe and RoW;
  • the five therapy buckets;
  • acquisition contributions.

It is thin on four things:

  1. Product-level revenue within "others", beyond the standalone percentages in the BRSR. The BRSR excludes the subsidiaries and uses a broader definition of infusion than the presentations.
  2. Customer concentration and the OEM/private-label share. Section A2 sets out what the placement documents and rating reports disclose.
  3. Country-level export revenue. The US is the only country for which management gives a number: exports of $3.5-4mn a year.
  4. Volume and price split by product. Management has occasionally given total devices sold (960mn units in 9MFY25, up 19% yoy against consolidated revenue growth of 23.2%) but not by line.

These gaps shape the question bank in Part C.

A2 How Polymed makes money

Polymed earns its money from:

  • Volume. It sold 960mn units devices in 9MFY25.
  • Low unit prices. Infusion devices sell at Rs. 10-20 each.
  • A cost base that it controls more tightly than most device makers. The group makes most of what it sells in-house, from moulds and tooling to automated assembly lines and, since 2025, gamma sterilization. On our calculation from the standalone accounts, FY26 costs as a share of revenue were:
    • raw materials 32.4%
    • employees 18.8%
    • job work 6.3%
    • power 3.4%
    • freight 2.0%

Standalone operating EBITDA margin was 26.8%.

  • Exports: 64.2% of standalone revenue was earned in foreign exchange, and foreign-exchange outgo equalled 46% of those earnings, a partial natural hedge (our calculation).

What sets Polymed apart among Indian device makers is not any single product. It is the combination of:

  1. scale in one high-volume device;
  2. in-house automation;
  3. a distributor network across 125+ countries that has been built over two decades.

The unit of demand: consumption, not capital spending

Almost everything Polymed sells is used once and discarded, so demand follows clinical activity rather than hospital budgets:

  • one IV cannula per insertion, re-sited every few days;
  • a pair of fistula needles and a bloodline set per haemodialysis session;
  • one blood bag per donation;
  • one feeding tube, urine bag or drain per patient episode.

The product glossary in the appendix sets out the unit of demand for each line.

This makes revenue recurring and less cyclical than equipment revenue. Its weaknesses are:

  • Low prices: most items sell for tens of rupees, not thousands.
  • Tenders: buyers can switch suppliers at tender time.
  • Channel inventory: stock held by distributors, which drove the FY26 slowdown (Section A3).

The shift into cardiology, orthopaedics and oncology changes the unit economics. Management says a single device there sells for thousands of rupees, sometimes above Rs. 20,000. That means fewer units, more clinical selling and a longer path through regulatory approval.

Price and volume: most growth has come from units

Management has occasionally split growth into price and volume:

  • FY22: volume contributed 75% of growth and price 25%.
  • 9MFY25: devices sold grew 19% against consolidated revenue growth of 23.2%.

The placement document describes the pricing pattern for mature products: average selling prices decline over a product's life, 'partially offset' by volume and cost reductions. Raw-material and currency changes are passed on to customers 'at periodic intervals', which means with a lag.

In India, prices of notified devices that are not under a ceiling cannot rise more than 10% in any 12 months under Para 20 of DPCO 2013, and MRPs are subject to an active trade-margin debate (Section B3).

For FY27 management took price increases of 3-5% to offset a ~20% rise in crude-linked raw materials.

The value chain: Polymed makes most of what it sells

The in-house machine shop is the least visible and most important part of this chain. Management attributes Polymed's cost position to it. It lets the company add capacity at lower capital cost than buying lines from European or Japanese machine builders, and it is the lever management cites against the Haryana minimum-wage increase: 'we are pushing more on automation right now'.

The main outsourced stage is job work. 84% of it went to Vitromed Healthcare in FY24 and 84% in FY26. This is a promoter-owned partnership, discussed under governance.

Channel and customers: distributors abroad, a direct sales force at home

Exports. All sales outside India go through distributors.

  • Network. Polymed had 260 overseas distributors at Jun-2024 (up from 215 in FY22). Many hold local or regional exclusivity 'subject to certain conditions', usually under two-year agreements.
  • Ordering. Distributors buy on purchase orders. Management notes there is no significant order backlog and no long-term customer contract, and customers 'often undertake vendor rationalisation'.
  • Direct presence. Management's Vision 2030 plan is to go direct in three or four European markets and in Brazil for new product ranges. At present it sells direct only in Italy, through Plan1Health.

India. Polymed runs its own field force alongside distributors.

  • Scale. At Jun-2024 it had 440 sales staff covering 8,000 hospitals and 506 distributors; the FY26 annual report cites 590+ sales representatives.
  • Customers. Management names corporate chains (Apollo, Max, Fortis, Manipal, Aster DM), diagnostic laboratories (Thyrocare, Metropolis, Dr Lal PathLabs) and dialysis chains (NephroPlus, DCDC) among its customers.
  • Government business. It has been shrinking: down 10% in 1QFY26 and 14% in 2QFY26, while private-sector sales grew.

Brand versus OEM.

  • Split. About 72% of FY22 revenue was sold under the PolyMed brand and 28% as OEM or private label. Exports ran at ~75% own brand, and management describes India as entirely branded.
  • Target. The stated aim was to move as close to 80% own brand.
  • Buyers. The OEM customers are not named. CRISIL's 2023 report says Polymed supplies 'other big players'.

Concentration is low.

  • Top ten customers: 27.6% of FY22 revenue, 29.0% in FY24 and 31.5% in 1QFY25.
  • Largest customer: between 4.9% and 7.3%.
  • Threshold: no customer has reached the single-customer disclosure threshold in any year since FY19.

The top customers are unnamed. Given the distributor model, they are presumably distributors.

Costs and margins: materials, people, job work, power and freight

How to read the cost table

  • Calculation. All ratios are our calculations from the standalone financial statements, each cited in its tooltip.
  • Operating EBITDA. For FY13-FY24 we deduct other income from the EBITDA printed in the directors' report. FY25 and FY26 use the company's operating EBITDA, which already excludes it.
  • Materials. 'Materials consumed' excludes purchases of traded goods and inventory changes, so it runs below the raw-material share the company quotes. The placement document gives 38.2%, 38.0% and 33.8% of total revenue for FY22-FY24.

Four patterns stand out.

  1. Materials set the cycle.
    • The materials ratio jumped to 37.7% in FY22 on polymer and freight inflation, and operating margin fell to 24.1%. Management had cut its FY22 EBITDA guidance twice.
    • It recovered to 33.1% by FY24.
    • Two features make Polymed exposed: 74.0% of materials were imported in FY23, and the company holds ~2.5-3 months of raw-material cover. A crude spike therefore reaches the P&L one to two quarters later, and price increases follow with a further lag. This is the sequence management described for FY27.
  2. Employee cost is the next pressure point. At 18.8% of FY26 revenue it is Polymed's second-largest cost. Haryana's minimum wage rose 35% from 1-Apr-2026, and standalone employee cost rose 29% yoy in 1QFY27. Management's answer is more automation.
  3. Job work has fallen steadily from 9.0% to 6.3% of revenue as in-house automation took over labour-intensive steps. This is the clearest evidence in the accounts of the automation strategy at work.
  4. Freight is now a visible cost. It ran at 0.7%-0.9% of standalone revenue over FY19-FY24 and reached 2.0% in FY26 (our calculation). Management says logistics costs have risen 2-3 times in the last six months.

Currency. Polymed invoices exports mainly in euros and US dollars. It imports most raw materials, and management says borrowings are 'primarily U.S. Dollar denominated', so a weaker rupee helps on net.

  • Unhedged exposure. At 30-Jun-2024, unhedged foreign-currency exposure was Rs. 1.6bn against forward contracts of Rs. 639mn.
  • No forward cover at FY26 year-end. At 31-Mar-2026 no forward contracts were outstanding, against a net unhedged US-dollar receivable of Rs. 2.4bn. FX gains of Rs. 381mn were booked in other income in FY26, below operating EBITDA, so the reported operating margin does not capture the full currency benefit.
  • Management's view. It cited rupee depreciation as a partial offset to FY27 cost inflation, since 60-70% of the business is international.

Export incentives. Export incentives (RoDTEP and drawback) were Rs. 133mn in FY26, well below the Rs. 167mn earned in FY19 under the earlier MEIS scheme.

Working capital: a long cycle by design

Exporting through distributors across 125+ countries ties up capital.

  • Receivables. Days fell from 80 days in FY22 to 73 days in FY24.
  • Inventory cover. Raw-material cover is ~2.5-3 months.
  • Cash conversion cycle. On the definition in the company's presentation, the standalone cycle lengthened from 183 days in FY25 to 215 days in FY26. Management quotes 140 days on its own measure.

Management attributes the FY26 rise in receivables to 'better terms' extended to international customers to hold volumes, and said it expects the receivable cycle to stay 'similar' in FY27. We treat receivable days as a leading indicator of distributor stress: a further rise alongside weak export growth would point to channel stuffing rather than a demand recovery.

A3 The FY26 reset and the guidance record

FY26 broke a run of on-guidance delivery.

  • Headline growth. Consolidated revenue grew 12.3% to Rs. 18.8bn, against the 20% management guided in May-2025. Standalone revenue grew only ~4%.
  • Organic growth. Excluding the part-year contribution of PendraCare and Citieffe, consolidated revenue grew 5.4% (our calculation on the company's organic figure of Rs. 17.6bn).
  • The miss was in exports. Domestic revenue grew 19.6% (vs. 30-32% guided). International revenue grew 9.3% including acquisitions but was 'flattish' on an organic basis, and management put 4QFY26 standalone exports at minus 3-4%.

The contrast with FY25 frames the debate. That year standalone revenue grew ~22.5% against 22-24% guided, and management said it had 'very accurately' forecast revenue.

Two readings of FY26 are possible:

  1. an external shock layered on a destocking cycle in the export channel;
  2. evidence that the export franchise is more exposed to Chinese pricing, US tariffs and regulatory friction than the FY21-25 record suggested.

The 1QFY27 print supports the first reading more than the second. Standalone revenue grew 12.3%, and Europe grew 17.6% organically on new customers. We nonetheless see three company-specific elements that matter for FY27-30:

  1. European certification and tender slippage;
  2. price competition in renal from Chinese dialysers imported duty-free via ASEAN;
  3. a guidance practice that moved targets without always acknowledging it.

What happened, quarter by quarter

The reset ran in three phases:

  1. Southern Europe slowed in 4QFY25. Management linked this to 'supply chain disruption, maybe overstocking' and uncertainty over US tariffs.
  2. Europe contracted in 1HFY26: -6.7% yoy in 1QFY26 and -9.6% in 2QFY26. Management gave these reasons:
    • distributors cut inventory from four to five months of cover to two to three months;
    • the reopening of the Suez route shortened transit from about two and a half months to about one month, releasing pipeline stock;
    • Chinese product was redirected from the US into Europe;
    • EU MDR certificate renewals came through only in Apr–Jun 2025.
  3. Recovery from 3QFY26, helped by the consolidation of the acquisitions. Europe grew 25.7% in 3QFY26 and 22.9% in 4QFY26 as reported. Management also cited National Health Service contracts in the UK delayed by one to one and a half years after the change of government, and products dropped during the MDR transition.

In India, private-sector revenue grew 25% and 22.3% in 1QFY26 and 2QFY26, while government business fell 10% and 14%. Management also pointed to a one-off inventory realignment at distributors around the September 2025 GST rate cut.

The guidance record

Management gives explicit annual guidance and revisits it every quarter, so the record is easy to score.

  • FY22-FY25: revenue guidance was met or beaten every year. The growth was built on a mix that routinely missed: domestic revenue fell short of guidance in FY23 and FY24, renal revenue missed in every year from FY22 to FY24, and US revenue targets slipped by years.
  • Management's method: it describes starting 'with a moderate number' and revising after the first or second quarter.
  • FY26:
    • Revenue guidance was cut from 20% to 15-16% in Nov-2025, then missed.
    • Renal and domestic guidance were missed by a wide margin.
    • 2HFY26 revenue was guided at 25%+ above 1HFY26 in Nov-2025, restated in Feb-2026 as 20% ('as I mentioned in the previous call'), and described in May-2026 as 'exactly delivered' at 21%.
  • FY27 standalone: the revenue guidance was widened from Rs. 19.0-19.5bn in May-2026 to Rs. 19-20bn in Aug-2026, while being described as maintained.

We read these as signs that guidance is framed as a target to be managed toward rather than a forecast with a stated error band, so we weight management's quarterly commentary on drivers more heavily than the point guidance.

What management is guiding for FY27 and FY30

FY27 guidance (given 25-May-2026 and 'maintained' on 10-Aug-2026):

  • Consolidated revenue: Rs. 23-24bn, including a full year of PendraCare and Citieffe. That implies 22.6%-28.0% growth on FY26 (our calculation).
  • Standalone revenue: Rs. 19-20bn, with domestic revenue growing over 20% and international revenue over 15%.
  • EBITDA margin: standalone 25-27%, consolidated 23-25%.
  • Capex: Rs. 2.0-2.25bn.

Vision 2030 ('PolyMed 3.0'): double revenue by FY30. Management characterized the current business as around 80% organic business and 20% inorganic business, and expects a similar split. An analyst on the same call worked this out as about 14-15% a year organic and 3-4% from acquisitions, which the MD described as 'a trajectory basically'.

The FY27 plan rests on four things:

  1. a recovery in Europe that 1QFY27 has begun to show;
  2. a normalization of West Asia shipping, which 1QFY27 has not;
  3. renal growth despite Chinese price pressure, which management is contesting through an anti-dumping petition;
  4. a full-year contribution from the acquired businesses, whose margins management puts below the group's.

What would change our read

Evidence that FY26 was structural rather than cyclical would include three things:

  1. Organic export growth staying in single digits once West Asia logistics normalize.
  2. Renal revenue declining for a second year without an anti-dumping duty.
  3. Receivable days staying above the FY26 level, which management attributed to 'better terms' extended to international customers.

Evidence that it was cyclical would be:

  • organic Europe growth holding in the high teens through FY27;
  • renal volumes recovering after a DGTR (Directorate General of Trade Remedies) determination.

A4.1 Infusion therapy

Six product families, one clinical workflow

A patient on an IV drip uses most of Polymed's infusion range at once:

  • a cannula in a peripheral vein;
  • an administration set from the bag, sometimes with a burette or flow regulator;
  • stopcocks and extension lines at the bedside;
  • a needle-free connector on the hub, capped with a disinfecting cap between uses;
  • saline flush syringes before and after every intermittent dose.

The unit of demand differs by item. It is one cannula per insertion attempt, one set per line, and several flushes per catheter per day. Demand therefore scales with admissions, IV-therapy days and protocol intensity rather than with hospital capital budgets. Exhibit 16 sets out each family.

The IV cannula: the anchor product

What it is. A peripheral IV cannula is a short plastic catheter over a steel needle. After the vein is punctured the needle is withdrawn and the catheter stays in the hand or forearm. Polymed sells gauges from 14G (colour-coded orange) to 26G (violet, for neonates). Its range covers four design generations:

  1. Conventional, ported or non-ported. Ported designs with a top injection port are standard in India, Europe and emerging markets; non-ported designs dominate the US.
  2. Safety, with a needle-tip shield that engages automatically (passive: Polysafety, Polywin Safety) or at the press of a tab (active: Polyguard+, Polyshield). These protect staff from needlestick injuries and are what regulated markets now buy.
  3. Blood-control safety (Polysafety BC), with a valve that stops blood flowing back once the needle is out. US demand has moved toward these.
  4. Closed-system (Nouvo Safety Set), with a pre-attached extension, a needle-free connector and a stabilizing platform, power-injectable to 300 psi for CT contrast.

Quick-flashback "Adva" needles, which show blood as soon as the tip enters the vein, run across the range.

Why volume keeps growing. Up to 90% of hospitalized patients receive a peripheral catheter, and published failure rates run as high as 69%, from phlebitis, infiltration, occlusion and dislodgement. Each failure means a new insertion, so cannula volume rises with admissions, day-care and emergency visits and with how often hospitals re-site. Better catheters that fail less reduce units per patient but command higher prices.

Scale and position. Management's claim has firmed over time:

  • "Amongst the Top 5" IV cannula makers in the world (Oct-2019 deck), then "Amongst the Top 3" from Oct-2020;
  • almost 10% of global volume (Jul-2024), then almost 11% on ~600mn pieces (May-2026);
  • the third-largest maker globally in the Aug-2026 deck, though the Sep-2026 AGM deck says only "amongst the largest".

No third party verifies the ranking. The two management figures imply a world market of ~5.5bn units a year (our calculation). That is far above the 1.5bn units a year quoted in a 2024 clinical-trial record. We think the higher figure is more plausible: B. Braun alone says its Penang site makes over 600mn catheters a year and that it has produced 10bn IV catheters since 1984. But the gap is wide enough that the share claim should be treated as indicative.

Revenue. The cannula is the only infusion product with a stated revenue share:

  • Now: 25-30% of revenue, in the MD's words (Jul-2024). On FY24 revenue that is between Rs. 3.4bn and Rs. 4.1bn, or 37% to 45% of infusion revenue (our calculation).
  • History: under the old Schedule VI the standalone accounts itemized IV cannula sales: Rs. 915mn in FY12, Rs. 1.1bn in FY13, Rs. 1.5bn in FY15 and Rs. 1.5bn in FY16. On our calculation the share of standalone revenue fell from 44% in FY12 to 37% in FY16.
  • CRISIL's figure: CRISIL Ratings put the "IV cannula segment" at 56-57% of FY22 revenue. That is close to the whole infusion segment's share (60.0% of FY22 revenue from operations in the placement document), so we read it as infusion, not the cannula alone, and use management's 25-30%.

Unit economics. Standalone Polymed sold 1.4bn units in FY25 (+24.7% yoy) for average revenue of Rs. 11.2 per device, against Rs. 11.4 in FY24 (our calculation). Management's ~600mn cannulas would be roughly 42% of those units (our calculation; the two figures are a year apart). The five largest products, which the company does not name, earned Rs. 9.6bn in FY25, +22.3% yoy.

Prefilled flush syringes: a second product family of renal-sized scale

The FY26 BRSR disclosed, in its life-cycle-assessment table, that PolyFlush prefilled saline syringes earned 10.2% of FY26 standalone turnover. That is ~Rs. 1.7bn (our calculation), close to the whole renal business (Rs. 1.9bn consolidated). It makes the flush syringe Polymed's largest product family after the cannula on the disclosed evidence.

  • Product. Ready-to-use syringes of normal saline in three fill sizes, all on the barrel diameter of the largest size, so that injection pressure stays low enough not to damage catheters. Single-sterile and sterile-field versions are offered, and the product is gamma sterilized.
  • Unit of demand. One flush before and after each intermittent dose plus scheduled flushes of idle lines, so several per catheter per day. Use is heaviest in US and European hospitals and home infusion; Indian adoption is lower.
  • History. Launched in Nov-2020. By May-2022 management called Polymed the only Indian manufacturer, with BD the only importer, and claimed ~25-30% of the Indian market. Exports had begun by Feb-2022. Management named prefilled syringes as a driver of product-sales and export growth in FY24 and FY25.
  • Competition. BD sells prefilled flush syringes within its Medication Delivery Solutions unit ($4.6bn revenue in FY25). In China, Weigao's flush-syringe prices fell sharply after the latest volume-based procurement round, one reason its group gross margin fell to 46.3% in the first half of 2026. We read this as a risk that Chinese flush syringes seek export markets, as Chinese makers have done in other disposables.
  • US access. None of Polymed's 6 US 510(k)s covers the flush syringe, so the US market is not yet open to it. In the US, Chinese syringes (HS 901831) have faced higher Section 301 duties since 2024 and FDA import alerts since May-2024.

Administration sets, stopcocks, extension lines and connectors

These are sold with the cannula and follow its channel, but their demand drivers differ.

  • Administration sets follow IV-line days and protocol changes. Specialist sets carry more value per unit: auto air-stop (Autofusion), paediatric micro-drip (Microfusion), light-protective (Photofusion) and oncology in-line filter (Oncofusion). Polymed has held a US 510(k) for Polyfusion sets since Apr-2023.
  • Stopcocks and extension lines are ICU- and theatre-heavy. Lipid-resistant stopcocks (Polyway Pro) tolerate propofol, and high-pressure lines serve syringe pumps and contrast injectors. Management named "three way stop cock" among the growth products of the foreign subsidiaries in FY24. The stopcock is also sold to other device makers as a component (Section A4.10).
  • Needle-free connectors and disinfecting caps are consumed per lumen and per access. Demand depends on infection-prevention protocols, which are strongest in the US and Europe, so these are export products for Polymed.
  • Competition in sets and connectors comes from pump makers and connector specialists. BD and ICU Medical sell dedicated sets with their infusion pumps, and ICU Medical says its pumps let it sell "a larger volume of higher margin infusion consumables". ICU Medical's Clave connectors and SwabCap caps, and BD's needle-free connectors and disinfecting caps, lead in the US. Polymed makes no pumps, so it competes in gravity and non-dedicated sets, and in flow regulators and burettes that stand in for pumps where hospitals lack them.
  • Scalp-vein sets were bought in from contract manufacturer Vitromed Healthcare at the time of the 2021 placement document; the 2024 document no longer says so. Whether any infusion products are still made on job work is [not disclosed] (Section A9).

Revenue: doubled since FY20, flat since FY25

Infusion revenue doubled from Rs. 4.7bn in FY20 to Rs. 10.0bn in FY26, a company-stated CAGR of 14%, but it has not grown since FY25:

  • FY26: -1.5% on the annual figures. The slowdown was driven by Europe, where distributors cut inventory, Chinese product diverted from the US undercut prices and MDR re-certifications arrived late (Section A3).
  • Mix: management described infusion as "a little bit a laggard" in 3QFY26, still growing "despite the weakness in international markets".
  • 1QFY27: infusion grew +11.1%, led by domestic infusion, up 20% plus on higher-value products and price increases, while West Asia fell.

Three measurement issues matter for anyone modelling this line:

  1. Annual and quarterly figures do not reconcile. The four FY26 quarters, as last reported, add up to Rs. 10.4bn, Rs. 399mn more than the annual Rs. 10.0bn. On summed quarters, FY26 growth was -1.1% (our calculation). The company moves some revenue from infusion into "others" in its annual tables and restated 1QFY26 from Rs. 2.5bn to Rs. 2.3bn.
  2. FY23 has two values: Rs. 7.7bn in the AGM deck and Rs. 7.3bn in the placement document's product table.
  3. The BRSR uses a broader definition. Its "Infusion Therapy" line was 69.9% of FY26 standalone turnover. That implies Rs. 11.6bn of standalone infusion revenue, Rs. 1.6bn more than the consolidated infusion revenue in the MD&A (our calculation). BRSR "Dialysis", by contrast, converts to Rs. 1.9bn, almost exactly the deck's renal revenue. The BRSR bucket therefore probably includes vascular-access and critical-care items that the deck puts in "others". We use the deck and MD&A series for infusion and quote the BRSR only for product families the deck does not show.

Where it is sold. Infusion is overwhelmingly an export business. Management put it at 90% of export revenue in Feb-2023 and ~80-85% for FY23, against ~45-50% of domestic revenue. That makes the line more exposed to European and US conditions than the group's 68% export share suggests. Standalone export sales to Europe fell from Rs. 5.4bn in FY25 to Rs. 4.9bn in FY26 and to the Americas from Rs. 2.9bn to Rs. 2.8bn, while Asia rose from Rs. 2.0bn to Rs. 2.3bn.

Customers, channel and pricing

Who buys. Abroad, every sale goes through distributors, several with national exclusivity. They supply hospitals directly or through tenders and purchasing groups.

  • Europe: Polymed wins on a wider basket per distributor and on price; management puts the price gap between Indian makers and multinationals at 20-25%. Six countries (UK, France, Italy, Germany, Spain and Sweden) produced one-third of export revenue in Feb-2022.
  • UK: the NHS buys ~90% of UK volumes. Polymed bid for 6-7 new product categories, mostly vascular, in the 2026 cycle, but management says NHS contracts slipped by 1-1.5 years after the change of government.
  • India: a direct sales force and distributors reach more than 8,000 hospitals, and all Indian sales are under the Polymed brand. Management expects hospital-chain consolidation to carry its products into merged chains.

Own brand versus private label. 25% of exports were white label in Feb-2023, and none of India's sales. The US business is being built through GPOs and distributors that "will be placing the product directly into the market", with Polymed's role "limited to manufacturing in India" (Section A4.10).

Pricing. Infusion devices sell at Rs. 10-20 each. Price is set against the multinationals above and the Chinese below, as Exhibit 19 shows.

Four points follow:

  • Regional price gaps are wide. The same BD device sells for roughly three times its European price in the US and a quarter of it in Africa (management's example). Polymed's move into the US is therefore a move into a higher price tier, not just more volume.
  • Price increases lag costs. Increases reach customers annually or at contract renewal, and in FY22 they reached only ~25-30% of customers. The FY27 increase of 3-5% compares with ~20% crude-linked raw-material inflation.
  • Rebates are rising. Standalone rebates and discounts rose to Rs. 116mn in FY26 from Rs. 73mn in FY25 and Rs. 53mn in FY24.
  • The incumbents' pricing is mixed. Price contributed -0.3% to BD's FY25 revenue growth, and BD blames "the entry into the market of low-cost manufacturers" and buying concentrated in GPOs. B. Braun's Hospital Care division did raise prices in 2025, and in the year to Mar-2026 Terumo's price increases added JPY 14.6bn to operating profit, more than offsetting a JPY 12.1bn hit from US tariffs.

In India, Para 20 of DPCO 2013 caps annual MRP increases on notified devices at 10% (Section B3).

The US: six clearances, small revenue, a moving tariff

The US is the largest market for safety and closed-system catheters and the highest-priced. Polymed's entire US 510(k) portfolio is in infusion.

The US effort has been slower than planned at every stage:

  • Approvals. 510(k) filings went in mid-2021. The FDA then asked for extra biocompatibility and genotoxicity testing, and in Nov-2025 management said the FDA had "started disapproving Indian lab results", forcing re-tests in the US.
  • Contracts. A contract with a large US distributor, signed in Aug-2022, was worth ~$15mn over five years for one product category. Contracts with two large GPOs and distributors followed.
  • Tariffs. The Aug-2025 US tariff on India was "completely untenable" and put customers on hold. After the cut announced in Feb-2026 management was "reinitiating conversations" with contracted customers. Section B4 sets out the legal path.
  • The channel. GPOs buy for more than 7,000 hospitals in the US (industry-body figure). ICU Medical warns that large rivals win "full-line" contracts to supply all of a hospital's or GPO's infusion needs, and that long-term fixed-price contracts stop it passing on cost increases; it paid $33.6mn in incremental US tariffs in 2025. A GPO contract gives access, not volume, since members "may choose to purchase from our competitors".
  • The next tariff risk. BD and ICU Medical both flag the Section 232 national-security investigation into imports of medical consumables and equipment, opened in Sep-2025, as a source of further tariffs whatever the country of origin.
  • Scale. The US subsidiary PolyHealth Medical booked only $211k of revenue in FY26. US sales run mainly as exports from India to distributors and GPOs, and management calls US exposure "not very big".

We see the US as an option on the safety and closed-system range rather than a driver of FY27 revenue. The evidence to watch is repeat GPO orders at US prices, not new clearances.

Europe and other export markets

Europe is Polymed's largest infusion market and the source of the FY26 slowdown (Section A3).

  • The downturn. Distributors cut inventory from four to five months of cover to two to three, and shorter transit after the reopening of the Suez route released more stock.
  • The recovery. Organic European revenue grew 17.6% in 1QFY27. Management attributes this to new customers, 5-6 of them from Apr-2026 and mainly in infusion, and to existing customers restocking.
  • MDR. The MDR cost Polymed products during the transition: "we had to drop many products initially". It now has ~75 CE-certified products, 20 new products filed for approval in 2026 and ~25 more awaiting CE marking. Every Polymed plant is certified under the EU MDR for its full range.
  • UK. The UK was 5-6% of revenue in FY25. The India-UK trade agreement cuts UK import duty on most of Polymed's products from ~4-5% to near zero.

Outside Europe, infusion sells through distributors in Latin America, Africa, Asia and West Asia, where currency and logistics set the pace. B. Braun reports stiffer competition in Latin America and Chinese share gains in ASEAN. West Asia revenue fell 32% in 1QFY27 because orders could not be shipped.

Market and competition

Market size (vendor estimates). Commercial research firms put the global peripheral IV catheter market at:

  • $5.74bn in 2025 (Research and Markets);
  • $5.1bn in 2026 (Persistence Market Research);
  • $7.5bn in 2026 (Future Market Insights).

They disagree on how much of it is already safety-engineered, from 30.3% (FMI) to 62% (Persistence). Persistence puts North America at 37% of the market. FMI names BD, B. Braun and Smiths (now part of ICU Medical) as the largest suppliers, and the reports list Terumo, Teleflex, Vygon and Nipro among the others. We treat these as ranges, not facts.

India. No source sizes the Indian cannula market on its own. 1Lattice, in a 2025 offer document, puts India's broader catheters-and-cannulae segment at $1.3bn in FY25, growing 9.1% a year over FY20-FY25 and making up 34.4% of Indian surgical disposables (vendor estimate). CRISIL says imports still meet 30-40% of Indian demand for consumables, less for needles and syringes and more for catheters and cannulas, so the room for import substitution lies in Polymed's core line. CRISIL put Polymed's share of India's whole consumables market at 1.9% in FY23, on domestic revenue of Rs. 3.4bn. Management sees the domestic runway in reach rather than market share: "I only covered one-third of the hospital[s]", the MD said in Feb-2025, and in Nov-2025 he put Polymed's wallet share in the hospitals it serves at 5-7%.

We draw four points from the competitor evidence.

  1. The global leaders are integrated systems sellers. BD and ICU Medical sell catheters with pumps, connectors and caps and compete for US GPO contracts across the whole portfolio. ICU Medical says it can now offer "a one-stop shop" and "more flexible competitive pricing". Polymed's closed-system and blood-control catheters, needle-free connectors and flush syringes are its answer to the breadth problem. Its lack of pumps is a gap.
  2. Chinese makers are shifting to exports, and towards Polymed's markets. KDL's core products (syringes, IV catheters, blood tubes, infusion sets) are now in provincial volume-based procurement at home, and China ran 78 such procurement projects for consumables in 2025. KDL's export gross margin was 35.6% against 31.5% in China. Chinese industry data show where the product went in 2025: device exports to the EU and UK rose 8.9% to $11.3bn, overtaking exports to the US, which fell 10.8% to $10.5bn, and exports to Latin America rose 13.3%. Weigao lifted overseas sales to 27% of revenue. B. Braun reports that in ASEAN "Chinese manufacturers expanded their market shares". Management describes Chinese device exports as "minus 20% in U.S. ... 25% to 30% in all other global markets".
  3. US policy now disadvantages Chinese syringes. The FDA told US providers in 2024 to move away from plastic syringes made by four Chinese firms, including KDL, and the US raised Section 301 duties on Chinese syringes and needles. Indian suppliers benefit, although management says US buyers have favoured Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia so far.
  4. Indian competitors are syringe-heavy, but some now match the cannula range. Hindustan Syringes claims capacity of more than 4.5 bn units per annum of disposables across eight Faridabad plants, but its newest plant was built for only 36mn IV cannulas a year, against Polymed's ~600mn. Smaller makers now offer the same catheter generations: Lars Medicare lists safety, blood-control and closed IV catheters and sells as a contract supplier to distributors in more than 100 countries (its own claims). CRISIL names Hindustan Syringes, Lotus Surgical, B. Braun and BD with Polymed as the key consumables players in India. Private equity is funding the domestic rivals: Temasek took a minority stake in Romsons in Sep-2026.

Polymed's differentiation, on management's account, is design (the group holds 390+ granted patents; "A lot of companies do copy our products"), MDR and FDA approvals that most Indian peers lack, and prices 15-20% below BD and B. Braun. In 2024 Polymed was pursuing a criminal complaint against B. Braun Medical (India) over a catheter patent.

Regulation and policy specific to infusion

  • Sharps safety. The EU sharps-injury directive and US needlestick legislation make safety-engineered catheters the default purchase in those markets. In India adoption is rising in private hospitals, and each hospital that converts moves from a conventional to a safety cannula at a higher price. CRISIL lists safety IV cannulas and safety vein sets among the safety devices now used in Indian infusion therapy.
  • EU MDR has raised the cost and time of keeping each catheter variant on the market. Management said in May-2022 that a CE mark took 18-24 months, against six to twelve before.
  • US. The 510(k) route applies, and the FDA now expects testing in US labs. Tariffs are set out in Section B4.
  • India. Price increases on notified devices are capped at 10% a year. Class A/B licensing has applied since Oct-2022 and Class C/D since Oct-2023, which management says has cut unregulated imports.

Capacity and supply chain

Polymed does not disclose which plants make cannulas or the capacity dedicated to them. In Aug-2023 the MD described the plant roles:

  • the Faridabad EOU extension serves the US business and new export products;
  • the Jaipur SEZ is "mainly for European business";
  • IMT Faridabad serves India.

The FDA listings for its safety IV catheters are all at a Faridabad establishment.

At group level:

  • Capacity rose from ~1bn units in FY20 to ~1.9bn units in FY26. Management put utilization of the post-2021 capacity at 65-70% in May-2026.
  • New plant: the Palwal plant, due around Mar-2027, adds infusion capacity alongside orthopaedics and transfusion.
  • Materials: the key inputs are plastic granules, FEP compound for catheter tubing and stainless-steel needle tube, mostly imported and bought on purchase orders (Section A2). Whether Polymed grinds needles in-house is [not disclosed].

Growth drivers, ranked

  1. India conversion and reach (management target; our view: most visible). Domestic infusion grew 20% plus in 1QFY27, with private hospitals (90% of domestic revenue) growing while Polymed exits low-margin government tenders. The levers are corporate-chain contracts, a larger clinical sales force and conversion to safety and closed catheters at higher prices.
  2. Europe restocking and new customers (management target; timing FY27). The evidence is 1QFY27 organic Europe growth of 17.6%. Management expects new customers from Apr-2026, MDR approvals and NHS contracts to add to it.
  3. Flush syringes, connectors and caps (our view). These are consumed several times per catheter, sell at higher prices in the US and Europe, and let Polymed sell a bundle rather than a cannula. The flush syringe is already ~Rs. 1.7bn of standalone revenue (our calculation).
  4. US safety catheters (management target; our view: an option, FY28 at the earliest). The US tier prices several times above Europe. Tariffs, the FDA testing issue and GPO ramp-up have delayed every US target since 2022.
  5. China-plus-one sourcing (our view: two-sided). The cost case is real: hourly manufacturing wages are below $2 per hour in India against $5-6 per hour in China (Frost & Sullivan), and 1Lattice reports that "many companies are moving manufacturing from China to India". US measures against Chinese syringes help. Chinese makers redirecting product to Europe and emerging markets hurt, as FY26 showed, and some are now producing outside China: Weigao's Indonesian joint venture is due to start local manufacturing in the second half of 2026.

Margin drivers, ranked

Polymed does not disclose infusion's margin. Management says exports earn "slightly higher" margins than India; in Feb-2023 it put export EBITDA at 30%+ against 18-20% for India (as transcribed, the answer sits inside the analyst's turn). Infusion is the most exported line, so it is probably the group's highest-margin line, but that is our inference.

  1. Automation. Assembly machines rose from 200+ (Aug-2025 deck) to 530+ (May-2026), and job work fell as a share of revenue (Section A2). Management's stated answer to Haryana's wage increase is "more automation".
  2. In-house gamma sterilization. It replaces outsourced ethylene-oxide sterilization, and management cites both quality control and savings. Capacity is going from 300 kci to 2,000 kci.
  3. Mix. Safety, blood-control and closed catheters, flush syringes and connectors earn more per unit than a conventional cannula. Average revenue per device slipped from Rs. 11.4 to Rs. 11.2 in FY25 (our calculation), so the mix gain is not yet visible in group numbers.
  4. Raw materials and freight (the main risk). Plastics are crude-linked and priced with a lag. Freight rose sharply in FY26 even though export prices are quoted ex-factory.

What to watch

Open questions for management (also in the question bank):

  • the IV cannula's revenue and volume by year, and the split between conventional, safety and closed catheters;

  • which plants make cannulas, and whether needle grinding is in-house;
  • the reclassification between infusion and "others" in the annual tables;
  • the basis for the global share claim and the third-place ranking.

Plan1Health: the Italian base

History.

  • The company: Plan1Health, of Amaro in north-east Italy, dates from 1995 on Polymed's slides. The 2024 placement document says it was incorporated in 1990.
  • The purchase: Poly Medicure B.V. paid EUR 3,468,600 and EUR 761,400 on 18 June 2018 and 6 March 2019, booking goodwill of €3.83mn. That is why Polymed's documents date the deal both FY18 (Rs. 342mn, placement document) and 2019 (enterprise value ~€4.2mn, company slide).
  • Scale: it had 31 employees in CY25.

What it makes. Management describes Plan1Health as a maker of long-term infusion devices, oncology products and "an innovative pain-control device", and as "a Class III implantables specialist and sole approved supplier to one of the largest OEMs for Class III implants" (Section A4.10).

  • Europe: it sells through 20+ distributors, with its whole portfolio of four categories approved.
  • US: its only clearance is the Painfusor catheter (K111031, 2011), which the company describes as an approval held for an OEM.
  • Italy: it gives the group its only direct sales operation in Europe.

Growth and margins. Plan1Health's revenue fell 5% in the COVID year, then grew 30% (FY22), ~50% (9MFY24) and 40%+ (FY24). Management said it was still growing 30-35% in Feb-2026.

The margin record is less clear, as Exhibit 25 shows. Management has described the EBITDA margin as "almost 16-17%" for FY24 and "around close to 16%-17%" in Nov-2025, yet its own acquisition slide shows 10.9% for CY24 and 13.4% for CY25. The statutory Italian accounts give an EBIT margin of 10.2% in CY25 (our calculation) and net profit of €681k. We use the slide figures, which fit the statutory EBIT, and treat the higher figures as management rounding or a different definition.

The company's own slides also disagree on the margin gain since the acquisition: 970bps (1QFY27 deck), ~670bps (Sep-2026 AGM deck) and ~600bps (4QFY26 deck). The 1QFY27 deck also claims 3.2x revenue and 11.8x EBITDA growth since 2020.

Links with the parent.

  • Trade: Polymed sold goods worth Rs. 147mn to Plan1Health in FY26.
  • Management: Plan1Health's founder and chief executive, Alessandro Balboni, also runs Polymed's EMEA business and sits on its board as a non-independent director. He was paid management fees of Rs. 29mn in FY26 (Section A9).
  • The "P1H playbook": management presents Plan1Health as the model for PendraCare and Citieffe: faster launches, manufacturing moved to India to cut cost, direct sales, wider distribution and joint R&D.

Oncology in Vision 2030 and in India

Management lists oncology among the five focus areas for the next five years, with orthopaedics, cardiology, neonatology and renal care, because these carry higher gross margins than infusion.

The Indian plan has four strands:

  • Local manufacture. Oncology products are sold in India through the critical-care team. Products "currently made in Italy" are to be moved to India, where management says demand is 80-90% imported.
  • Higher-class devices. Chemoports, a Class III implantable, have been launched in India. Management frames this as a shift from short-term devices to "longer term use devices" in Class III, with competition mostly from multinationals and "a couple of good Indian companies also like Meril".
  • Public cancer care. The government is setting up standalone specialty oncology (day-care) centres: 200 in FY26 and 700 over three years, on management's account.
  • New products. Recent launches include neonatal PICCs of 1Fr & 2Fr ("one of the first" made in India), an antimicrobial CVC, tear-away introducers, midlines, PICC-ports and a safety Huber needle.

Market and competition

Commercial research firms (vendor estimates, used as indications only) put these global markets at:

  • implantable ports: $1.4bn in 2025;
  • PICCs: $1.43bn in 2026;
  • central venous catheters: $3.07bn in 2026;
  • catheter introducer sheaths: $1.93bn by 2030.

Their company lists put B. Braun, BD, Cook Medical, AngioDynamics, Smiths Medical and Teleflex in ports and PICCs. Poly Medicure is listed among PICC and CVC suppliers.

The large competitors are:

  • BD: ports sit within its Peripheral Intervention unit ($2.0bn revenue in FY25), and PICCs and midlines within Medication Delivery Solutions.
  • Teleflex: its Arrow-branded vascular catheters generated $917.7mn in CY25.
  • ICU Medical: it sells Port-A-Cath ports, PowerWand midlines and Deltec Gripper non-coring needles within a consumables business of $1.1bn.

In India, management names multinationals and Meril as the competitors in oncology implants.

We see Plan1Health's advantage as regulatory, not scale. It holds MDR approval for its whole portfolio and supplies a Class III OEM, and management says each country registration takes two to three years. Polymed's contribution is lower-cost manufacturing and a distribution network in 125+ countries.

Regulation specific to the line

  • Class III. Ports are Class III implants, the highest risk class in Europe. Management says Class III devices take "very much, much longer to register", often two to three years including clinical trials.
  • Italian payback. A 2015 Italian law requires device suppliers to pay the government when regional device spending exceeds annual ceilings; the Constitutional Court upheld it in Jul-2024. BD booked a $62mn accrual for it in FY24 and settled at 25% of invoiced amounts. Italian public spending on medical devices and services is about €10bn a year, according to the industry association Confindustria Dispositivi Medici. Plan1Health sells directly to Italian hospitals, so it is presumably exposed, but its exposure is [not disclosed].
  • US. Beyond Painfusor, Plan1Health has FDA submissions "under evaluation".

Growth and margin drivers, ranked

  1. India import substitution in ports and PICCs (management target; our view: largest long-run lever). The drivers are chemotherapy volumes, the day-care cancer centres and hospitals preferring local suppliers as insurance spreads. Timing depends on moving Plan1Health products to Indian manufacture and approval.
  2. Plan1Health's European growth (management: 30-35% a year). The levers are the new MDR filings for oncology, neonatal and pain products and cross-selling Polymed products through Plan1Health in Italy.
  3. Recurring consumables per port (our view). Huber needles and maintenance items follow the installed base of ports.
  4. Margin: scale and Indian manufacturing (company target). The company-reported EBITDA margin rose from 3.7% to 13.4% as revenue tripled. Management expects moving labour-heavy processes to India to add more.

Critical care: the growth project

Management's account of critical care has moved:

Why management likes the segment. It argues that critical care has few global competitors ("Cardiology ... 20 companies ... Critical Care is only 4-5 companies"). Indian demand is almost entirely imported (80-90%, Oct-2024), and ICU patients consume many devices each: management put ICU beds at ~20% of hospital beds. The segment is also the channel for oncology products (Section A4.2).

We read critical care as a cross-selling engine for devices Polymed already makes, sold by clinically trained staff, rather than a new product category. Its success should therefore show up in the domestic "others" line and in private-hospital growth, not in a separately reported number.

Market and competition

Market data are thin and vendor-based. Research and Markets pages put the global endotracheal-tube market at $2.54bn in 2026 and anaesthesia face masks at $252.42mn in 2025 (vendor estimates).

The competitive set is consolidating:

  • Teleflex agreed in Dec-2025 to sell its Acute Care business to Intersurgical for $530mn, as part of a $1.5bn sale of its OEM unit and other divestitures. Acute Care covers airway management, anaesthesia, respiratory, humidification, oxygen therapy and regional anaesthesia. Teleflex had already sold respiratory lines to Medline in 2021.

In India, management says imports supply most of critical-care demand. CRISIL notes that Indian makers mostly produce basic items such as nasal oxygen cannulas, oxygen masks, ventilator circuits and mucus extractors, and that multinationals and imports dominate the consumables market.

Polymed's position in the basic range rests on price and breadth within the same distributor basket as infusion. In critical care it rests on local manufacture against imports and on clinical selling. We see no evidence of a share lead in any single product here.

  2. Surgical volumes and caesarean-heavy spinal-needle demand (our view). This is the base-load driver of the anaesthesia range.
  3. Export of Class I items to the US (our view: small). Polymed already lists them, and private label is one route.
  4. Margin (our view). Anaesthesia and respiratory items are commodity-like, and we read them as earning below the group average. Critical-care devices replace imports and management says they carry "a better margin". Neither margin is disclosed.

The line is still small, at 5% of 1QFY27 revenue, and its two engines are both running below plan. In-house DES volumes in FY26 were about half of what management planned. We see less of it in the stent, where prices are capped and Indian makers already lead.

What the devices do and the unit of demand

A coronary angioplasty (percutaneous coronary intervention, PCI) consumes a basket of devices.

  • Access: an arterial needle and an introducer sheath.
  • Delivery: a guiding catheter seated at the mouth of the coronary artery, and a guidewire run through it.
  • Treatment: semi-compliant balloons to open the narrowing, a stent to hold it open, and non-compliant balloons to expand the stent fully.
  • Accessories: an inflation device, a Y-connector valve and a manifold, often sold together as a kit.

A diagnostic angiogram needs diagnostic catheters and wires only. A DEB delivers drug to the vessel wall without leaving a stent, and IVL cracks calcified plaque before a stent is placed. The unit of demand is therefore the procedure. Management estimates that India performs ~700,000 angioplasties a year and uses ~1.3mn units of coronary stents, or 1.6-1.7 stents per patient. It puts annual sales at ~50,000 units for DEBs and ~10,000 units for IVL.

Polymed's range: a full cath-lab basket, built in two years

Polymed entered cardiology in 2024 with a soft launch at Arab Health. Its DES licence came in Feb-2025 and the stent went on sale in Mar-2025. DCGI approved its DEB and IVL systems in 3QFY26. By the Sep-2026 AGM the DEB was on sale and the IVL system was "soon launching". Management says both were developed on its own R&D platform.

Who makes which device is [not disclosed]. No legal manufacturer is printed on any ordering table or product page, so we cannot tell which items come from Polymed's plants, which from PendraCare and which are bought in. The Nov-2024 components catalogue already offered a cobalt-chromium stent in sirolimus and everolimus versions, together with diagnostic catheters and guidewires, as parts for other device makers. Frost & Sullivan's Jul-2025 report for Sahajanand Medical Technologies (SMT) still listed Polymed only as a PTCA-balloon maker "most focused on consumables".

Traction: stent volumes, the registry and the approval clock

Cumulative deployments reached ~11,000 units by 30 Apr 2026, so FY26 volumes were roughly half of the close to 20,000 units management planned in Aug-2025. Management says the stent is a small business, worth Rs. 100-120mn of FY26 revenue. It uses the stent as a door-opener for diagnostic catheters, guidewires and balloons. The stent is still sold mainly outside the top-tier hospitals, which management says it has not yet entered.

The PACIFIER registry is the evidence base for taking the RisoR stent abroad.

  • Design: 2,000 patients (1,960 in India and 40 in Europe) at 50 Indian sites and one in Italy, with imaging sub-studies of 100 (QCA) and 50 (OCT) patients, over 24 months. It is an open-label, single-arm registry, which management describes as post-market evaluation in India and "pre-market readiness" for Europe.
  • Enrolment: 200+ patients by Feb-2026, 650+ by May-2026 and 840+ by Aug-2026, against a target of full enrolment by end-FY27. The design had assumed a one-year enrolment window.
  • Our arithmetic: at the Feb-Aug 2026 pace of ~107 patients a month, the remaining 1,160 take ~11 months, which runs past end-FY27 (our calculation). Data follow at least a year after the last implant, and management says Class III implants take 3-5 years to win approval in global markets. We therefore do not expect European sales of the RisoR stent before FY29.
  • Disclosure quality: the 4QFY26 deck printed 2,000+ patients enrolled on one slide and 650+ on another.

PendraCare: what Polymed bought, and what has happened since

The deal. Polymed bought 90% of PendraCare Holdings and Wellinq Medical from Wellinq Holdings. The buyer was Risor Holdings BV, which is owned by Poly Medicure BV.

  • Price: an upfront equity payment of €11mn plus €3.2mn of inter-company loans repaid, a total of €14.2mn. Polymed also took on €2.9mn of net debt.
  • Valuation terms: an enterprise value of €18.3mn at first closing, or €18.8mn in the 1QFY27 deck. That is 1.8x CY24 revenue and 13x CY24 EBITDA (company).
  • Deferred terms: milestone earn-outs run over 4-5 years. The CEO's remaining 10% is bought in 2030 at a price set on calendar 2029 EBITDA; neither amount is disclosed.
  • Accounts: the annual report records Rs. 1.5bn paid in cash and Rs. 314mn of contingent consideration, with provisional goodwill of Rs. 1.6bn.
  • Funding: Rs. 1.5bn came from the Aug-2024 QIP and was lent on to Risor by Poly Medicure BV (€14.6mn at a floating rate linked to Euribor).

The business. PendraCare was founded in 2011 and describes itself as one of the few independent makers of interventional-cardiology catheters.

  • Products: guiding catheters were 79% of CY24 core revenue and diagnostic catheters 21%. It also sells semi-finished catheter parts and designs and makes braided catheters for other companies.
  • Channel: 48% of revenue came from OEM customers and 52% from its own brand, sold through 50+ distributors in 35+ countries with no direct sales force. Its OEM relationships with 2 large device companies go back more than a decade.
  • Regulatory assets: registrations in 60+ countries and 4 US 510(k)s, the latest for the SimplGuide introducer in Jun-2026. Its guiding catheter has 2.5 years of ARCUS-IMPROVE clinical data.
  • Plant: 3,800 m² across three leased sites in Leek with 1,150 m² of clean room. Capacity is 1.5 mn units/year against production of 700-800 thousand units. The staff is 65+ people.

Four points stand out.

  1. The CY25 collapse. Revenue fell 17.2% and EBITDA margin went from 14.1% to 1.0% (our calculations; the AGM deck prints CY25 gross profit of €6.3mn, not ~€5.59mn). The company has not explained the fall. On the 1QFY27 call management pointed to an "over-indexed" exposure to West Asia of about 20-odd percent. It said the business is "currently not growing", has withheld FY27 guidance for it and has not disclosed its 1QFY27 growth.
  2. The post-deal accounts are worse than the deck. Over the roughly six months after the deal, Risor's consolidated revenue of €4.17mn did not cover costs, and employee cost alone ate 65.6% of it. EBITDA was a loss of €752k and the net loss €1.33mn (our calculation from the unaudited accounts). Risor was set up with €20k of share capital and funded by the Poly Medicure BV loan, so the loss turned its equity negative. In the consolidated accounts, PendraCare added Rs. 442mn of revenue and a pre-tax loss of Rs. 141mn.
  3. Targets are far from delivery. Management promised €3-4mn of annual EBITDA synergies within three to four years, and revenue of close to €25mn by 2030 including new neuro and structural-heart products. CY25 EBITDA of ~€84k makes both look distant.
  4. Housekeeping conflicts. The EV is printed as €18.3mn, ~€18mn and €18.8mn in different documents. Risor's incorporation date is 29 Aug 2025 in the annual report but 4 Nov 2025 in Poly Medicure BV's notes, after the Sep-2025 loan to it.

The integration plan has four parts.

  • Move manual process steps to India; management said this was at the regulatory-approval stage in Aug-2026.
  • Consolidate the three Leek sites into one ~2,300 m² lease in calendar 2028. The new site will need its own approvals.
  • Start US sales of the guiding catheter in FY27 through distributors and a global OEM.
  • Register PendraCare products for import into India, which management says takes 6-9 months.

Market: a price-capped stent market and an import-heavy accessory market

Three features of the market shape Polymed's opportunity.

  1. Stents are price-capped. NPPA's 2017 cap cut patient prices by 60-80% according to CRISIL, and by 85% according to Frost & Sullivan. Price increases since then have been limited to the wholesale price index.
  2. Indian makers already hold the stent market. Management puts the Indian share at 70% today, against 10% a decade ago, while Frost & Sullivan says about 60%. A new entrant therefore wins stent share on price or service, and management concedes that peers "already have clinical trial data". The import-dependent parts of the basket are catheters, specialty balloons, DEB and IVL.
  3. Procedures should keep growing. India performs a small fraction of rich-country PCI rates per head, and 200-250 cath labs are added each year. Management says the stent market has "almost doubled" in five years, and expects DEB and IVL volumes to grow 2-3-fold in three to five years.

Globally, Frost & Sullivan's report for Integris sizes interventional cardiology at $14.3bn in 2024. It puts catheters at $3.6bn, growing 10.6% a year, and PTCA balloons at $2.9bn. The same firm's report for SMT gives $1.3bn for PTCA balloon catheters, which shows how much these vendor figures depend on definitions.

Customers, channel and pricing

  • India: Polymed sells to cath labs through its own field force and distributors. It added 70 people to cardiology and critical care in FY25 and trains cardiologists at its PACE academy.
  • Price points: the DEB and IVL carry ASPs above Rs. 115,000, against a stent price of Rs. 10,000-12,000. Management says pipeline products will be priced between Rs. 100,000 and Rs. 1.0mn.
  • PendraCare: it sells through distributors and OEM partners. Management rules out a direct sales force for now and plans to put PendraCare products into Polymed's Indian sales bag.

Competition

  • Stents: Abbott, Boston Scientific, Medtronic, Teleflex, Biosensors and Terumo hold over 90% of the global DES market.
    • In India the leaders are SMT (~25% of DES volumes), Integris/Translumina (~22%), Meril, Micro Life Sciences and Relisys.
    • The MD calls Meril the one Indian peer with a comparable spread of businesses, and notes that it entered cardiology more than a decade before Polymed.
  • Accessories, DEB and IVL: Frost & Sullivan's Oct-2025 list shows that Integris already sells guiding and diagnostic catheters, guidewires, inflation devices, introducer sheaths, Y-connector kits and coronary IVL. Polymed is therefore not alone in the import-substitution play it describes.
  • Guiding catheters worldwide: PendraCare competes with the large multinationals, some of which also buy from it as OEM customers. Management counts several times as many competitors in cardiology as in critical care.

Regulation

  • India: DES and bare-metal stents sit under NPPA ceiling prices. Other cath-lab devices do not, but their MRPs are subject to the annual price-increase limit that DPCO Para 20 sets for notified devices (Section B3).
  • Europe and the US: management says approvals for implantable devices take years and that EU MDR has made them "extremely challenging". It called PendraCare's CE and FDA clearances the main reason to buy rather than build. Polymed's own cardiology devices have no US 510(k); the group's cardiology clearances all belong to PendraCare.

Capacity and supply chain

  • India: cardiology capacity will move to a new plant on a plot of 7.2 acres in the YEIDA Medical Devices Park near the new Noida airport, where commercial production is guided for 1QFY28 (Section A6).
  • Netherlands: PendraCare runs at about half its capacity, and management puts both acquired businesses at 50-60% of capacity.

Growth drivers, ranked

  1. DEB and IVL (management target, FY27 onward). These products are import substitutes priced above Rs. 115,000, sold into a market of ~50,000 units of DEBs and ~10,000 units of IVL systems a year that management expects to multiply. We rank this first because the ASP is high and India has few local suppliers, though Integris is ahead in IVL.
  2. Basket pull-through (our view). A catheter, wire, balloon and kit accompany every stent. Winning top-tier hospitals would lift revenue per procedure more than stent volume would.
  3. PendraCare in the US and as a contract maker (management target, FY27). The guiding catheter is cleared and sales are expected from FY27. This depends on the OEM partner, which is not named.
  4. RisoR abroad (our view, FY29 at the earliest). This depends on completing PACIFIER, a year of follow-up and a European approval.
  5. YEIDA capacity (1QFY28, management guidance). The plant matters only if demand catches up with the stent plan.

Margin drivers, ranked

  1. PendraCare's recovery. It moved from a 14.1% EBITDA margin (CY24) to 1.0% (CY25). Management says the combined acquired businesses run at 12-14% and can reach 18-20% in two to three years through India engineering, global procurement and shifting manual steps to India.
  2. Mix. DEB, IVL and PendraCare's gross margin (73.7% in CY24) sit well above capped stents.
  3. Front-loaded costs. The registry, clinical teams and a new sales force are expensed ahead of revenue, and the government has granted Rs. 33mn towards clinical studies.
  4. Euro translation for PendraCare.

What to watch

Open questions for management are in the plant-visit question bank (Part C). They include which cardiology devices are made in-house, PendraCare's CY25 EBITDA bridge and the terms of the earn-out.

What it is and how it is used

Citieffe makes implants that hold broken bones in place while they heal.

  • Intramedullary nails: metal rods run down the hollow centre of the femur, tibia, humerus or fibula and locked with screws. This is the standard fix for many long-bone shaft fractures.
  • External fixators: frames outside the limb, attached to bone by pins, for open or complex fractures and limb reconstruction.
  • Screws and plates: cannulated screws for fractures such as the femoral neck, and plates for fractures such as the wrist.

The unit of demand is the fracture: one nail and its locking screws per long-bone fracture, or one frame per complex fracture. The pins and screws are single use, while frame parts are re-used in some markets. Implant sets usually reach hospitals on consignment with instrument trays, and the navigation system is placed with hospitals to support nail sales. Trauma surgery cannot be postponed. Industry data in the acquisition deck show the trauma and extremities market fell 6.1% in the Covid year, against 11% for orthopaedics as a whole.

Citieffe's portfolio and position

  • Company: Citieffe was founded near Bologna in 1962 and has been run since 2019 by CEO Pascal Govi, who has 34 years of experience. It holds 45 patents, has an R&D team of 6 FTE spending ~5% of revenue, and launches 3-5 products a year. It reports a very low defect rate.
  • Position: it is the number-two independent player in Italy with a share of ~12% excluding plates, and one of the top players in Mexico with ~12% excluding plates and low-cost products. Both are company estimates.
  • Reach: ~85% of sales come from the three countries where it sells direct: Italy, the US and Mexico. It sells in 30+ countries in total. The distributor count is printed as 25+ in the 1QFY27 deck but 50+ in the annual report.

The deal

The deal was signed on 24 Sep 2025, closed on 6 Nov 2025, and Citieffe was consolidated from 7 Nov 2025. The multiple of 10.2x was struck on CY24 EBITDA of €3.1mn, which the company describes as excluding one-off expenses and income.

Financials: flat sales, a net loss and a long receivables tail

Five points stand out.

  1. Growth stalled. The company-presented revenue grew 2.3% in CY25, after 15% in CY24. Management said sales were flat because a tender Citieffe had won in Latin America was cancelled and pushed out. It guided 10-12% euro growth for FY27, but on the Aug-2026 call described Citieffe as growing at "mid- to high single digits".
  2. Two EBITDA figures. The company's EBITDA is ~€3.15mn for CY25. Adding back D&A and finance cost to the audited IFRS profit before tax gives €2.72mn (our calculation), and the same gap existed in CY24. We read the difference as the one-off items the company excludes. It matters because the price was set on the adjusted figure.
  3. The loss was mostly currency. The CY25 net loss of €1.21mn came after an FX loss of €1.26mn on the US and Mexican businesses. Interest fell to €423k from €659k. The CY24 profit also shrank in a restatement: the deferred tax asset on Citieffe Inc's US tax losses had been booked at the gross loss rather than the tax-effected amount, and the correction of €3.43mn cut equity.
  4. Receivables are stretched. 51% of gross receivables were more than 120 days old at Dec-2025, up from 44% a year earlier. The loss allowance covers only 24% of that overdue book (our calculations). Management says the acquired European businesses carry longer receivable cycles.
  5. Debt stays with the business. Interest-bearing debt of €13.1mn equals about 100% of equity (our calculation), backed partly by SIMEST and export-credit agency guarantees. Management puts the core loan at ~€9mn, repaid at €1.5-2mn a year. Italian overdrafts carry rates up to double digits.

Inside Polymed, Citieffe contributed Rs. 726mn of revenue and a pre-tax loss of Rs. 1.0mn between 7 Nov 2025 and 31 Mar 2026, and ~Rs. 430-440mn of 4QFY26 revenue. Management blamed part of the acquired businesses' ~Rs. 26mn EBITDA drag in 4QFY26 on Citieffe "selling certain low-margin products".

Market: trauma is the fastest-growing orthopaedic segment

  • Global: the acquisition deck puts orthopaedics at $61bn in 2024, growing 3.8% a year to 2028. Trauma and extremities is the largest and fastest-growing segment at $12bn, growing 6.3% a year to $16bn by 2028. Internal fixators are 82% of the segment and external fixators 13%, and the US is 67% of the orthopaedic market. Plates are almost 40% of the trauma market. These are vendor estimates quoted by the company.
  • India: the research firm engaged for G Surgiwear's DRHP (Dec-2025) values India's orthopaedic device market at $6.2bn in FY25, rising to $10.7bn by FY30 (11.4% a year), of which implants are $3.0bn. India is about 3% of the global market. These are vendor estimates.
  • Import dependence is disputed. CRISIL put implants at 80-90% imported in FY24, while management says 50-60% of orthopaedic implants are now made in India.

Customers, channel and pricing

  • Buyers: trauma surgeons and hospitals. Citieffe's own sales teams cover Italy, the US and Mexico, alongside 12 distributors in Europe and 50+ key opinion leaders worldwide.
  • US: Citieffe sells to trauma centres through independent agents rather than group purchasing contracts. Management ruled out selling Polymed's consumables through the orthopaedic reps.
  • Tenders: in Italian and Latin American public tenders a bidder often needs a full portfolio, which is the main argument for adding plates.
  • Prices: [not disclosed]; management declined to give product prices. The company says gross margin is above 90%+, while the 1QFY27 deck implies 68% (our calculation).

Competition

  • Multinationals: Stryker, DePuy Synthes, Zimmer Biomet, Smith+Nephew, Medtronic and Arthrex lead worldwide and in India's premium segment.
  • Indian makers: Meril, Sharma Orthopaedic, Narang Medical, Biorad Medisys and Bonetech sell cost-effective trauma, joint and spine implants.
  • Management's view: India has "a fairly large base of manufacturers" in trauma, and Zydus's recent French acquisition is mainly knee implants.
  • Citieffe's edge: Citieffe competes on design (its patents, navigation for nailing) and on a low complaint rate rather than on price, and it has no plates to offer in tenders.

Regulation

The whole Citieffe portfolio is certified under EU MDR. Its products are Class IIb in Europe and Class II in the US. Registration in India's import regime takes 6-9 months, according to management. India capped the MRPs of coronary stents and knee implants in 2017; trauma implants were not named in that order.

Capacity and supply chain

  • Plants: two sites in Bologna: a production plant of 1,000 m² with 12 machines, and an assembly and packaging site of 1,100 m² with 10 machines. They run at 60% of capacity, with room to add a third shift.
  • Sterilization: outsourced, with a plan to bring it in-house.
  • Capex: regular capex is €1.5-2mn a year including instrument sets, on a net block of ~€5mn. Management estimates the total invested in the business at ~€28-30mn.
  • Staff: 109 employees at the deal and 90+ in Aug-2026, although management says no staff cuts have been made at either acquisition.
  • India: at the deal, management said it had "no such plans" to move manufacturing to India, only to outsource parts of the process. By Aug-2026, moving manufacturing processes of both acquisitions to India was "at the regulatory approval stage". The new Palwal plant in Haryana, due online around Mar-2027, will focus on orthopaedics and transfusion (Section A6).

Growth drivers, ranked

  1. Plates (management plan, near term). Plates are almost 40% of the trauma market, and management says adding them doubles the addressable market and qualifies Citieffe for full-line tenders. No launch date or approval timeline has been given.
  2. US sales force (management plan). Citieffe has 10 US approvals with 8-10 more in the pipeline, and prices there are 4-5 Europe's. More agents should lift the smallest share of the sales mix.
  3. Polymed's distribution and an India unit (management plan, from FY27). This means registering Citieffe products for India and selling through Polymed's network in more countries.

The evidence on OEM and private label

Other sources add detail:

  • Management's framing has shifted. The Oct-2020 deck pitched "new contract manufacturing opportunities". In May-2023 the MD said "Polymed doesn't have any contract manufacturing business today. And that's not our model". By 1QFY26 it had signed two CDMO contracts.
  • By market. Europe "is more favoured towards own branded", while the US leans to white label.
  • The acquisitions raise the OEM share. PendraCare makes semi-finished guiding and diagnostic catheter parts, licenses end-product finalization and does contract development and manufacturing of braided catheters for 2 large OEMs, with relationships of over 10 years. The Vitromed Healthcare relationship runs the other way. Polymed pays this promoter-owned firm to make products on job work from Polymed's raw materials; it is not a customer. It is covered in Section A9.

Why it matters: margins, stickiness and concentration

  1. Margins. Private label usually sells below own-brand prices because the customer carries the brand and the selling cost, but Polymed does not disclose the gap. Management put export EBITDA at 30%+ in Feb-2023, against 18-20% for India (as transcribed, the answer sits inside the analyst's turn). Since exports carry nearly all the private label, the mix does not appear to have hurt export margins, but this cannot be tested.
  2. Stickiness. OEM supply is embedded in the customer's regulatory file: changing a component supplier means re-validation and often re-registration. Management says most of its customer relationships outside India run 15-20 years and that it has "hardly lost any large customer".
  3. Concentration. OEM revenue follows a few customers' production schedules. At group level concentration is low: the top ten customers were 29.0% of FY24 revenue and no single customer reached a tenth of sales (Section A2). The OEM customers are not named, so their weight within the top ten is [not disclosed].
  4. Strategic value. In Dec-2025 Teleflex agreed to sell its OEM business, which makes catheters, extrusions, introducers and guidewire-type components for other device makers, to Montagu and Kohlberg for about $1.5bn. That shows the OEM model can stand as a business in its own right. Polymed's components catalogue covers many of the same categories at a far smaller scale. India's CDMO opportunity is small today (<$100mn in 2025, per a study cited in the FY26 annual report) and is projected at $6-7bn by 2035.

We read Polymed's stance as pragmatic: own brand where it can build a franchise (India, Europe), private label and CDMO where access is otherwise hard (the US). The CDMO contracts are the first test of whether it can earn design fees, not just manufacturing margins.

A8 Competitive advantages and vulnerabilities

Polymed's advantage is a manufacturing one:

  • It makes high-volume disposables at a lower cost than Western majors and at a quality that clears European and US regulators.
  • It sells them through a distributor network in 125+ countries.

This is why the company earns Indian-peer margins while exporting 69% of revenue. The weaknesses sit elsewhere:

  • Pricing power. It has little brand premium.
  • R&D depth. It spends 1.6% of revenue on R&D.
  • Chinese competition. It is exposed to Chinese makers with similar cost structures and larger scale.
  • The US. It is barely present in the largest market.

The acquisitions and the stated plan to double R&D spend are attempts to fix the second and fourth of these.

Where the advantage comes from

  1. Unit cost through automation and in-house engineering.
    • Polymed runs 530 automatic assembly machines and 130 robots, up from 200 machines in Jun-2024. It designs its own moulds and builds many of its own machines.
    • CRISIL attributes the company's operating margins to lower labour costs than global peers and to in-house tool design and R&D.
    • A peer comparison in Integris Medtech's draft offer document puts Polymed's FY25 EBITDA margin at 28.7% (gross margin 66.8%). That is the highest EBITDA margin among the companies it compares: Integris Medtech, Poly Medicure and Laxmi Dental.
  2. Scale in the core product. Management says Polymed is the world's #3 IV cannula maker, with about 11% global share and 600mn units a year. Scale spreads tooling, automation and regulatory costs over large volumes.
  3. Vertical integration.
    • The company makes components, moulds and assembly machines in-house.
    • It commissioned in-house gamma sterilization in 2025.
    • It is India's first domestic maker of dialysers and its only maker of prefilled flush syringes.
  4. Regulatory breadth.
    • 89 products carried EU MDR certification in FY25.
    • Products are registered in 100+ countries.
    • The company employs 200+ quality and regulatory professionals.
    • Polymed and its subsidiaries hold US 510(k) clearances across infusion, vascular access, cardiology and orthopaedics (Appendix).

MDR has raised the cost of staying in Europe, which works against smaller suppliers (Section B4).

  1. Distribution reach.
    • 1,000+ distributors and 590 direct-sales representatives.
    • No customer reaches the 10% disclosure threshold, and the top-10 customers were 29.0% of FY24 revenue.
  2. A steady launch cadence.
    • About 35 products new products in FY26, 390+ patents granted and a pipeline of 100+ products.

Most launches extend existing categories rather than open new ones.

Where it is vulnerable

  1. Pricing power is limited.
    • CRISIL notes that India's low-cost image "limits the company's ability to command a premium".
    • In Europe, Polymed competes with Chinese makers of similar cost and larger scale. Weigao alone had revenue of RMB 13.4bn in CY25, 27% of it overseas.
    • FY26 showed how quickly redirected Chinese volume can reach European tenders (Section A3).
  2. R&D depth.
    • R&D spending is 1.6% of revenue. The pipeline is broad but mostly incremental.
    • Management's answer is to buy technology and approvals, because organic development takes 3-5 years. That shifts the risk from R&D to integration.
  3. Under-penetration of the US.
    • US exports are $3.5-4mn a year. By comparison, BD's medication-delivery business alone had revenue of $4.6bn in FY25.
    • Without GPO contracts and US-registered supply, Polymed's cost advantage cannot reach the largest market.
  4. Concentration in infusion and in Europe.
    • Infusion is still 53% of revenue.
    • The BRSR puts infusion therapy at 69.9% of standalone turnover on its broader definition.
  5. Imported inputs and labour intensity.
    • Most raw materials are imported.
    • Permanent-worker attrition was 74.6% in FY26, and 51.1% of permanent workers are paid the minimum wage.

Wage inflation erodes the labour-cost edge unless automation keeps pace.

What would strengthen or erode the advantage

Strengthen:

  • automation keeping employee cost flat as a share of revenue despite wage inflation;
  • the new plants lowering unit costs in renal and syringes;
  • FDA clearances and US contracts;
  • MDR-driven exits by smaller European suppliers;
  • trade remedies against dumped imports in India.

Erode:

  • Chinese makers relocating to Southeast Asia to escape US and Indian duties;
  • MNCs pricing aggressively in emerging markets;
  • a cap on trade margins in India;
  • slow integration of acquisitions that dilutes management attention away from the core franchise.

A9 Management, governance and capital allocation

Polymed is a founder-run company. The Baid brothers built it, still run it, and with their family and affiliated entities hold 62.4% of the equity.

The record has real strengths:

  • clean audit reports, with no auditor qualification in the five years to FY24;
  • a CRISIL rating of AA-/Positive;
  • a net cash balance sheet;
  • two QIPs that institutions absorbed.

It also carries a set of governance features that we think any investor should diligence before underwriting the FY30 plan:

  1. Job work with a promoter firm. Rs. 870mn of FY26 job work went to Vitromed Healthcare, a partnership of promoter-family members. That was 84% of all job-work charges.
  2. A promoter group enlarged by reclassification. The reported holding moved from 44.7% to 62.4% after two LLPs that had been counted as public shareholders were reclassified. Neither step involved a purchase of shares.
  3. Promoter-family pay. It equalled 14.7% of standalone PAT in FY26 (our calculation), and the MD earned 497x the median employee.
  4. Slow deployment of the Aug-2024 QIP. Only 21% of the capex scheduled for deployment by FY26 was spent, and the monitoring agency flagged that part of the idle money sat in hybrid mutual funds and a PMS.
  5. A mixed early record for the FY26 acquisitions. PendraCare contributed a pre-tax loss of Rs. 141mn in its first six months under Polymed, while Citieffe was close to breakeven after closing.

None of these is hidden. Each is disclosed in the annual report, the placement documents or the monitoring-agency reports. None has drawn material institutional dissent: no resolution at the 31st AGM had more than 4.0% of institutional votes against it.

Who runs Polymed

The operating company is still run by its founders:

  • Himanshu Baid (MD), who joined on 20-Sep-1996.
  • Rishi Baid (JMD), formerly with Miles Pharma in the US. Both describe themselves as first-generation entrepreneurs.
  • Their parents on the board: J.K. Baid as a non-executive director and Mukulika Baid as a non-executive director.

The next generation is inside the business:

  • Vishal Baid is now an executive director, in charge of marketing, corporate finance and the Jaipur manufacturing operations.
  • Dhruv, Arham and Aaryaman Baid are employed in the company.

From 2025-26 a professional layer was added:

  • a CEO for India and APAC hired from Translumina, who earlier ran B. Braun in India;
  • a CEO for Brazil;
  • a head of the Indian renal business;
  • a President for strategy and corporate development.

The acquired businesses keep their own chief executives.

The board. At 31-Mar-2026 the board had 12 directors. Eight were non-executive, five of them independent, and one was an independent woman director.

  • Chairman. D.R. Mehta, a former SEBI chairman, has been on the board since 2005. He is now classified as a non-executive, non-independent director.
  • Re-appointment. Because he is older than 75 years, the 31st AGM re-appointed him by special resolution. 4.0% of institutional votes were cast against, the highest dissent of any resolution at the meeting.

With the promoter parents and the Plan1 founder among the non-executives, the board has a slim independent balance by the standards of widely held companies. It still meets the one-third test that applies to a non-promoter non-executive chairman.

Shareholding: how the promoter group reached 62.4%

The promoter group's reported holding has swung between 44.7% and 66.0% over five years with almost no change in the family's own shareholdings. The swings are almost entirely the result of two reclassifications:

  • Zetta Matrix Consulting LLP. It held 9.4% in FY20 as a public shareholder and was moved into the promoter group in FY23.
  • Ezekiel Global Business Solutions LLP. It held 14.0% in FY20 and was listed as a major non-promoter shareholder through 31-Mar-2024. By 30-Jun-2024 it was inside the promoter group.

The arithmetic ties exactly:

  • The reported promoter holding at 31-Mar-2024 plus Ezekiel's then-public stake is 66.03% (our calculation). That is the promoter-group figure the placement document reported at 30-Jun-2024 (66.0%), weeks before the Aug-2024 QIP.
  • In FY20 the reported promoter group and the two LLPs together held 72.2%. In FY22 they held 66.2%. The fall between those years is the dilution from the Feb-2021 QIP.

The documents we reviewed disclose neither who controls the two LLPs nor why they were reclassified. We therefore do not infer control. We do note three consequences:

  • The public float is 37.6%.
  • Public institutions held about 21.2% at the AGM record date (our calculation).
  • The effective free float is narrower than the pre-FY23 shareholding pattern implied.

Promoter shares carried no pledge at 30-Jun-2024.

  • What they avoid. Current-quarter trading, order-book numbers, unannounced transactions and changes to guidance.
  • If an answer strays into UPSI, stop the line of questioning and note it.
  • Numbers in the context lines come from public filings, presentations and call transcripts cited in this report.

How to read this report

Every number is traceable.

  • Each figure is drawn from a fact ledger built from company filings, presentations, call transcripts, rating-agency rationales, regulator texts and peer offer documents.
  • Hover over any number, or press on it on a touch screen, to see the source document, its date and the page or slide.
  • Figures computed by us (shares, growth rates, CAGRs, currency conversions) are labelled "our calculation", and the tooltip shows the formula and its inputs.
  • Market-research estimates from commercial vendors are labelled as such and used only as ranges.

Conventions.

  • Currency: rupee amounts are in Rs. mn and Rs. bn; company figures reported in crore or lakh are converted.
  • Periods: fiscal years end on 31 March (FY26 = April 2025 to March 2026) and quarters are written 1QFY27. European and other overseas subsidiaries report on calendar years (CY25).
  • Growth is year-on-year (yoy) unless stated.
  • Geography: "West Asia" refers to the region the company calls the Middle East.
  • "Standalone" is Poly Medicure Ltd, the Indian listed entity. "Consolidated" adds the subsidiaries and the equity-accounted associate.
