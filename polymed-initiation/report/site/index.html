Poly Medicure (POLYMED): India's export-scale disposables maker, now buying its way up the value chain

A low-cost, highly automated infusion franchise funds a push into renal, cardiology and orthopaedics. Execution on acquisitions, new plants and Chinese price competition is the test.

Polymed in 10 points

  1. What it is. Polymed makes single-use medical devices: IV cannulas and infusion sets, dialysis consumables and machines, blood bags and collection tubes, catheters for urology, surgery, anaesthesia and critical care and, more recently, cardiology stents and catheters and trauma implants. It runs 15 plants in 5 countries and sells in 125+ countries. It is the world's #3 maker of IV cannulas by its own account.
  2. How it makes money. It sells very large volumes of low-priced devices: management cites Rs. 10-20 per infusion device. CRISIL attributes its margins to a labour-cost advantage over global competitors and in-house tool design; we add automation and vertical integration. The standalone business earned a gross margin of 68.1% and an operating EBITDA margin of 26.8% in FY26 (Section A2).
  3. Infusion is about half of revenue, and falling as a share. Infusion was 53% of FY26 revenue, down from 68% in FY20, because renal and the other lines grew faster (Exhibit 1; the split by year is in Exhibit 4). Infusion revenue fell 1.5% in FY26 before growing 11.1% in 1QFY27. The statutory BRSR gives a finer split of standalone turnover:
    • in the "others" bucket: blood management and collection, 9.5%; surgery and wound drainage, 5.3%; urology, 3.0%;
    • within infusion: prefilled flush syringes, 10.2%, one of the largest single product families.
  4. About two-thirds of revenue is earned outside India. ~69% of FY26 revenue came from outside India, including the foreign subsidiaries' own sales. India, Europe and the rest of the world now each contribute about a third (Exhibit 7), and India has grown fastest since FY20 (Exhibit 1). The US is still small: management expects exports there of $3.5-4mn in FY27.
  5. FY26 was a reset. Consolidated revenue grew 12.3% (5.4% organic, our calculation) against initial guidance of 20%, and standalone revenue grew only ~4%. Management cited destocking by European distributors, Chinese product diverted from the US to Europe, faster Suez transit and late MDR renewals. Organic growth recovered to 12.4% in 1QFY27 (Section A3).
  6. Renal is the second engine, and the most contested. Polymed is India's first domestic dialyser maker and had about 1,000 dialysis machines installed by FY26. Renal revenue fell 3.8% yoy in 1QFY27; management cited Chinese price pressure and said it chose to raise prices rather than defend volume. Its anti-dumping petition, as summarized in DGTR's initiation notice, says the dialyser business made losses and a negative return on capital in CY25, and Polymed was not among the PLI incentive recipients listed to FY25 in a Jul-2026 parliamentary answer (Section A4.3).
  7. Acquisitions add cardiology and orthopaedics, with a weak start. PendraCare (Netherlands) and Citieffe (Italy) contributed Rs. 723mn of revenue in 1QFY27. PendraCare lost Rs. 141mn before tax in its first six months under Polymed; Citieffe was near breakeven. Cumulative deployments of its India-made stents passed ~13,000 by Jul-2026 (Sections A4.8, A4.9).
  8. Margin drivers are mostly company-specific.
    • Pressures: materials consumed (32.4% of FY26 standalone revenue, our calculation), a 35% increase in Haryana's minimum wage from Apr-2026, freight, and dilution from the acquired businesses.
    • Offsets: automation (530 assembly machines and 130 robots), mix and a weaker rupee.
    • See Sections A2 and A7.
  9. The industry backdrop is mixed.
    • Tailwinds: India's device policy (PLI, NMDP and procurement preference), EU MDR attrition among small suppliers, and US duties on Chinese syringes and needles.
    • Risks: a possible cap on trade margins in India (a Parliamentary committee has recommended limiting the landing-price-to-MRP gap to 20%), Chinese pricing in Europe and in renal, and US tariff volatility.
    • See Part B.
  10. Capital allocation and governance deserve diligence.
    • Unspent QIP money: Rs. 4.8bn of the Aug-2024 QIP was unused at 31-Mar-2026.
    • Job work with a promoter firm: 84% of FY26 job work went to promoter partnership Vitromed (our calculation).
    • A reclassified promoter group: two LLPs once counted as public shareholders were moved into the promoter group.
    • High executive pay: the MD earned 497x the median employee.
    • Offsetting strengths: clean audits, a CRISIL AA-/Positive rating and net cash of ~Rs. 5.0bn (Section A9).
