A4.10 Components, OEM and contract manufacturing

Polymed sells some of what it makes to other device companies rather than under its own name: finished products sold as private label, loose components and semi-finished parts, and, since 2025, design-and-manufacture contracts. The evidence is scattered, and the revenue is [not disclosed]. What we can piece together is that private label was ~28% of sales in FY22 and is concentrated in exports, that management wants it smaller, and that the acquired European businesses have brought a larger OEM share.

What Polymed offers other device makers

Components. The Nov-2024 components catalogue ("Empowering Healthcare Innovation") offers parts that another manufacturer can assemble, pack and sterilize under its own name:

  • catheters: diagnostic, CVC, haemodialysis, midline, mini-midline, PICC and anaesthesia conduction;
  • a coronary stent: cobalt-chromium with a biodegradable polymer, sirolimus or everolimus;
  • guidewires: nitinol straight and J-tip, stainless steel, PTFE-coated, hydrophilic, a nasogastric-tube guidewire and a guidewire advancer;
  • access parts: introducer needles (standard, safety and Y with valve), tear-away introducers and dilators;
  • infusion parts: Polyway stopcocks, flow regulators, T-connectors, and extruded tubing (radio-opaque, PVC-free, polyurethane, co-extruded).

Many of these items were in the components catalogue before Polymed listed them as branded cardiology products. The catalogue is therefore both a revenue line and a window into its capabilities.

The government has approved a grant of Rs. 100mn towards a Rs. 1.0bn Polymed project to make polymer tubing components for infusion therapy, bloodlines and dialysis accessories and central venous catheters (Section A4.1). That should deepen the extruded-tubing capability behind both the finished products and the components line (our view).

Private label. Finished products sold under a customer's brand, mainly abroad. The only documentary trace in the US is an FDA device listing at Jaipur for a suction catheter kit under the proprietary name "McKesson", a US distributor. Management says the US business will run through GPOs and distributors, with Polymed's role "limited to manufacturing in India".

Contract design and manufacturing (CDMO). In 1QFY26 Polymed signed two contracts, with a US company and a Hong Kong-headquartered company with large US operations. They cover new patented devices in vascular access and pain management, and 510(k) filings are in progress. Management expects revenue from FY27 and scale in "two, three years, four years".

Intra-group supply. Polymed supplies components to its subsidiaries, which management says creates "some margin creation at the India level". Sales to Plan1Health alone were Rs. 147mn in FY26.

The evidence on OEM and private label

Other sources add detail:

  • CRISIL Ratings wrote in 2023 that Polymed "supplies to other big players, such as Atroge and Asept" (names as printed). It said Polymed has "access to developed markets through contract manufacturing" but that India's low-cost image "limits the company's ability to command a premium".
  • Management's framing has shifted. The Oct-2020 deck pitched "new contract manufacturing opportunities". In May-2023 the MD said "Polymed doesn't have any contract manufacturing business today. And that's not our model". By 1QFY26 it had signed two CDMO contracts.
  • By market. Europe "is more favoured towards own branded", while the US leans to white label.
  • The acquisitions raise the OEM share. PendraCare makes semi-finished guiding and diagnostic catheter parts, licenses end-product finalization and does contract development and manufacturing of braided catheters for 2 large OEMs, with relationships of over 10 years. Plan1Health is the "sole approved supplier for one of the largest OEM for Class III implants".

The Vitromed Healthcare relationship runs the other way. Polymed pays this promoter-owned firm to make products on job work from Polymed's raw materials; it is not a customer. It is covered in Section A9.

Why it matters: margins, stickiness and concentration

  1. Margins. Private label usually sells below own-brand prices because the customer carries the brand and the selling cost, but Polymed does not disclose the gap. Management put export EBITDA at 30%+ in Feb-2023, against 18-20% for India (as transcribed, the answer sits inside the analyst's turn). Since exports carry nearly all the private label, the mix does not appear to have hurt export margins, but this cannot be tested.
  2. Stickiness. OEM supply is embedded in the customer's regulatory file: changing a component supplier means re-validation and often re-registration. Management says most of its customer relationships outside India run 15-20 years and that it has "hardly lost any large customer".
  3. Concentration. OEM revenue follows a few customers' production schedules. At group level concentration is low: the top ten customers were 29.0% of FY24 revenue and no single customer reached a tenth of sales (Section A2). The OEM customers are not named, so their weight within the top ten is [not disclosed].
  4. Strategic value. In Dec-2025 Teleflex agreed to sell its OEM business, which makes catheters, extrusions, introducers and guidewire-type components for other device makers, to Montagu and Kohlberg for about $1.5bn. That shows the OEM model can stand as a business in its own right. Polymed's components catalogue covers many of the same categories at a far smaller scale. India's CDMO opportunity is small today (<$100mn in 2025, per the BCG-KIHT report of May-2026 cited in the FY26 annual report), which projects it at $6-7bn by 2035.

We read Polymed's stance as pragmatic: own brand where it can build a franchise (India, Europe), private label and CDMO where access is otherwise hard (the US). The CDMO contracts are the first test of whether it can earn design fees, not just manufacturing margins.

Who else competes for OEM work

  • Chinese suppliers have long served the global majors. KDL, which ranks itself among China's ten largest exporters of polymer consumables, sells on OEM, ODM and own-brand terms and has long-term development and supply ties with several global device companies.
  • Chinese private label carries risk for the buyer. Jiangsu Caina made the syringes Cardinal Health sold in the US under its Monoject brand. The FDA found that Caina had received at least 41 US complaints, sent it a warning letter and by May-2024 was telling users to move away from Caina's syringes.
  • Indian contract makers are smaller. Angiplast offers CDMO and OEM services and makes components such as spikes, injection sites, connectors and clamps, and says it exports more than 80% of its production. Lars Medicare calls itself a contract OEM supplier. Iscon Surgicals sold to OEMs as well as to government buyers.
  • The opening. CRISIL says that pricing pressure and supply-chain problems during and after the pandemic have pushed OEMs to seek cost-competitive alternatives, making India an emerging hub for devices of high complexity and medium volume.

In our view Polymed's advantage over the Indian contract makers is its regulatory file: the company says every plant is certified under the EU MDR, and it holds 6 US 510(k)s (Section A4.1).

What to watch

Open questions for management (also in the question bank):

  • current own-brand and OEM shares, and the margin gap between them;
  • the names or types of the largest OEM customers, and their share of the top ten;
  • revenue from the components catalogue;
  • expected FY27 CDMO revenue and the status of the related 510(k) filings.
