A6 Manufacturing, capacity and R&D

Polymed makes almost everything it sells, and it makes it in India. In our view that is what lets it sell infusion devices at Rs. 10-20 each (the MD's illustration) and still earn a standalone operating EBITDA margin of 26.8% (FY26). The process is integrated from mould-making to sterile pack: moulds, dies and much of the assembly machinery are built in-house, parts are moulded and extruded on site, and products are assembled on automated lines. Sterilization began moving in-house in 2025, when the first phase of a captive gamma plant started. The next two years are about filling capacity rather than adding it:

  • capacity grew faster than output after FY22, and management puts utilization of the post-2021 capacity at 65-70%;
  • two new Indian plants (Palwal and the YEIDA medical-device park) and the second phase of the captive gamma sterilization plant are due on stream between end-2026 and 1QFY28;
  • wages in Haryana, where most of the plants are, rose sharply from Apr-2026.

R&D spend is low at 1.6% of turnover: the FY26 annual report itself puts Indian medtech firms at 2-4% of revenue and US firms at 10-15%, without citing a source. It has grown 16% a year since FY19 (our calculation). It now supports 35 launches a year and a pipeline of more than 100 products.

The plants: what is made where

Polymed counts 15 facilities in 5 countries. India has 10, which the annual report does not itemize: its footprint page pictures the R&D centre, the second Haridwar plant and the gamma plant alongside the production sites. The two Citieffe sites in Bologna count as one. The FY26 annual report lists nine Indian production units in three clusters: Faridabad in Haryana, Jaipur's Mahindra World City special economic zone (SEZ), and Haridwar in Uttarakhand. Plant-level product lists are not published. The table assembles what the calls, placement documents and the US FDA's registration database do reveal.

Three things in the table matter for a plant visit.

  1. The US plant is Unit II. All the FDA-cleared safety IV catheters are listed under the FEI at the export-oriented unit in Faridabad. The Jaipur SEZ, built for Europe, holds the only US private-label listing.
  2. Renal capacity sits at IMT. In Aug-2025 management said current renal capacity would last only to FY26-27, which is why renal capacity was part of the Palwal plan. By Aug-2026 Palwal was described as for orthopaedics, transfusion and infusion, and renal revenue had stalled (Section A4.3), so we read the constraint as less pressing.
  3. The new plants are for the new businesses. Palwal is for orthopaedics, transfusion and infusion; YEIDA is for cardiology. Management expects partial revenue from the new capacity in FY27 and full revenue in FY28.

Capacity and utilization

The only plant-level capacity data are the chartered-engineer tables in the two placement documents (PD21 for FY18-FY20, PD24 for FY22-FY24). Since then the company has given only group totals in its presentations.

What the series shows:

  • FY24 was a capacity year. Installed capacity rose 20% in FY24 while output rose 6% (our calculation), as four plants commissioned in FY23-FY24 came on line, and certified utilization fell (Exhibit 97). The same placement document's MD&A gives higher utilization for each year, a conflict it does not explain.
  • Presentation capacity has kept climbing: 1.2bn units (May-2023), 1.5bn units (Jul-2024), 1.8bn units (May-2025) and ~1.9bn units (Aug-2026). The presentations do not say which plants each figure covers.
  • Management's utilization statements are consistent with the certificates:
    • new plants start at 30-40%, take three to four years to reach 80-90%, and 80-85% is the practical peak;
    • in May-2026 capacity added since the 2021 QIP ran at 65-70%; the Jul-2024 and Oct-2024 readings are in the note to Exhibit 97.

The acquired plants have the same slack: management put both PendraCare and Citieffe at 50-60% of capacity in Feb-2026. Group plant area rose from 63,000 sq m in FY20 to 95,000 sq m in FY26.

Automation, tooling and the machine shop

Every Indian plant has injection moulding (including hot-runner tools), extrusion, insert and blow moulding, and ultrasonic, UV and laser welding, with poka-yoke, vision inspection and servo controls. Each plant has its own effluent treatment plant. Moulds are made on in-house CNC machines, and much of the special-purpose assembly equipment is designed in-house. Management said these special-purpose machines take 12-18 months to deliver, which sets the lead time for new capacity.

We read the step from 200+ to 500+ assembly machines between Aug-2025 and Nov-2025 as a change in what is counted, not a purchase. The acquisitions added only 12 and 10 machines at Citieffe's two sites. The definition is a plant-visit question.

Needles. Polymed's devices depend on stainless-steel needles and cannulae. The sources do not say whether Polymed draws, grinds and siliconizes needles in-house or buys finished needles. Chinese competitors such as Kindly (KDL) run the full chain from tubing to assembled device, so this is a gap in understanding the cost position.

Job work. Not everything is made in Polymed's own plants. Standalone job-work charges were Rs. 1.0bn in FY26, of which Rs. 870mn (84%, our calculation) was paid to Vitromed Healthcare, a promoter-group firm that assembles products from materials Polymed supplies (Section A9).

Sterilization: from outsourced EtO to a captive gamma plant

Until 2025 Polymed sterilized mainly with EtO gas through third-party vendors. The catalogues label some products gamma-sterilized (the PolyFlush syringe, Polyswab, the ABG syringe) and others EtO-sterilized (transducer protectors, peritoneal-dialysis sets).

The captive plant has been a long project:

  1. May-2022. Management added Rs. 500mn to the capex plan for 'a controlled radiation plant', because many new products are gamma-sterilized, there was no facility near the plants, and outside vendors sometimes compromised quality. It was due in FY24.
  2. Jul-2024. Still under construction; management called gamma 'more safer' than the EtO 'which we use today' and planned to move 'most of our sterilization' to it.
  3. Aug-2025. Phase I started, licensed for Class A and B devices, with Class C and D licences awaited; source capacity to rise from 300 kci to 2,000 kci.
  4. Sep-2026. 'Operational; one of the few such captive facilities in India', with Phase II due by end-2026.

Management places the plant variously at 'Palwal' and at 'Faridabad-Mitral'; the annual report says Mitrol. Why it matters: EtO is under regulatory pressure. About 50% of US sterile devices are EtO-sterilized, and the FDA is encouraging alternatives including radiation. In-house sterilization should also shorten the time from moulding to shipment (our inference). Neither the cost of the plant nor the savings, nor the share of output now sterilized in-house, is disclosed. Citieffe outsources its sterilization and plans to bring it in-house.

Capex and the QIPs

The capex cycle has run in two waves, each funded by a qualified institutional placement (QIP):

  • 2021 QIP: four plants in FY23-FY24. They were a diagnostics plant in Faridabad, an extension of the export-oriented unit for the US, an IMT expansion for domestic and renal products, and an SEZ expansion for Europe. This is the capacity now at 65-70% utilization.
  • 2024 QIP: three plants. Rs. 5.0bn was earmarked for plants at Jaipur (Rs. 2.3bn), Palwal (Rs. 2.7bn) and Haridwar (Rs. 790mn), out of a total project cost of Rs. 5.8bn. The planned plant and machinery (Rs. 3.1bn) comprised assembly machines (Rs. 1.2bn), injection moulding (Rs. 679mn), tool room and moulds (Rs. 554mn) and clean rooms (Rs. 322mn); 99.7% of it had not been ordered at Aug-2024. Jaipur has since been replaced by YEIDA. By 31 Mar 2026 only Rs. 620mn (12%, our calculation) of the capex object had been spent, while Rs. 2.5bn of QIP money had gone to acquisitions. Section A9 covers the change of use.

Management says its asset turns 'remain between' 1.2-1.4x and expects that to continue. Reported fixed-asset turnover fell to 1.6x in FY26; the annual report gives no reason, and we read it as capacity running ahead of revenue.

Workforce and wages

Headcount figures depend on the definition used. The annual report cites a group workforce of over 7,500 and an 'employee base' of 3,400+; the Directors' Report's count of permanent employees on the standalone rolls fell in FY26 while the BRSR's rose (Exhibit 101). The standalone BRSR gives the most complete picture.

Three implications:

  • Wage exposure is concentrated in Haryana. The Faridabad plants held 64% of group capacity in FY24 (our calculation), and Mitrol and Palwal are also in Haryana. Haryana raised minimum wages 35% from 1 Apr 2026, which, with a larger headcount, management said drove a 29% rise in standalone employee cost in 1QFY27. The new Labour Codes cost a further Rs. 68mn provision in 3QFY26. Consolidated employee cost was Rs. 4.0bn in FY26.
  • Automation is the stated offset. Management said in May-2026 that it is 'pushing more on automation right now so that at least we can mitigate some wage revisions'.
  • Contract labour fell 28% in FY26 (our calculation) while permanent headcount rose. Whether this reflects automation, lower volumes or reclassification is not disclosed.

R&D, patents and launches

  • People. The DSIR-approved centre in Faridabad had 63 staff including 59 engineers in Jun-2024. The group R&D team was ~90+, including 45+ engineers across India, Italy and the Netherlands, in May-2026. R&D employee cost rose to Rs. 150mn, 50% of R&D expense (our calculation). Citieffe adds a team of 6 FTE.
  • Ambition. Vision 2030 promises to 'double R&D spend' (2x). Management puts spend at 1.7% of sales and says it will double in three to five years. Indian medtech firms spend 2-4% of revenue on R&D, according to the FY26 annual report's MD&A, which cites no source.
  • Patents. The group holds 399 patents, of which 348 are Polymed's own, so the subsidiaries hold 51 (our calculation). Around 60 applications are pending. In FY25 the count was 334, including 116 in India, from 616 applications filed.
  • Clinical evidence. The 2,000-patient PACIFIER stent registry, which management calls the first stent registry of this scale in India, is the largest study Polymed has disclosed (Section A4.8). The government granted Rs. 33mn for clinical studies of new import-substitution devices. Management uses contract research organizations for Class III devices and says companies typically spend 3-5 years getting global approvals for Class III implants.

Approvals by market

What drives plant economics, ranked

  1. Filling the capacity already built (visible). At 65-70% utilization on the post-2021 plants against a practical peak of 80-85%, volume growth can come with little new capex; management says FY27 capex guidance is lower because the plants under construction are nearly ready. Mechanism: fixed cost absorption. Timing: FY27-FY28.
  2. Wages against automation (headwind now). A 35% minimum-wage rise in the state that hosts most capacity is only partly offset by automation; the net effect showed up as 29% employee-cost growth in 1QFY27.
  3. Captive sterilization (management claim, unquantified). Management's stated aims are control of quality and turnaround and a safer process than EtO; we would add the vendor margin and transport it removes. The benefit is undisclosed and depends on the Class C/D licences.
  4. New plants for new businesses (cost before revenue). Palwal and YEIDA add depreciation and fixed cost from end-FY27, ahead of the orthopaedics and cardiology revenue they are built for.
  5. Moving acquired manufacturing processes to India (management plan, early). Citieffe's and PendraCare's plants run at 50-60%. At the Citieffe deal management had 'no plans' to move its manufacturing, only to outsource parts of the process to India; by Aug-2026 some process transfers for both acquisitions were 'at the regulatory approval stage'. Our view: a margin lever for FY28 onwards, gated by approvals.

Open questions for the plant visit are in the question bank (Part C): which products each unit makes; current capacity and utilization by unit; how assembly machines are counted; whether needles are ground in-house; the share of output sterilized in-house and its cost saving; and what moves to Palwal from Citieffe and PendraCare.
