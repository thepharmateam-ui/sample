A4.3 Renal care

Renal is Polymed's second line and, until FY26, its fastest-growing one: a full haemodialysis consumables basket plus an Indian-made machine, sold into a market where the only other local dialyser plant is an export unit and where, management says, pricing is set by Chinese imports that enter duty-free through South-East Asia. The line's next leg depends on two things outside Polymed's control, a trade-remedy ruling and public dialysis tenders, and on one inside it, turning a small installed base of machines into consumables pull-through.

What it is and how it is used: one session, one set of consumables

In haemodialysis the patient's blood is pumped from an arteriovenous fistula in the arm through a dialyser, where toxins and excess water cross a hollow-fibre membrane into dialysate that the machine prepares from purified water and concentrates, and back into the vein. Each session, typically three to four hours, uses:

  • a pair of AV fistula needles (arterial and venous), always single use, the cleanest per-session consumable;
  • one bloodline set and usually two transducer protectors;
  • one dialyser, which Indian centres commonly reprocess and reuse for the same patient;
  • acid and bicarbonate concentrates, which Polymed's catalogues do not list.

Temporary access (acute kidney injury, or while a fistula matures) uses a haemodialysis catheter kit, and acute bedside peritoneal dialysis uses a stiff PD catheter set. A machine is a one-off sale that then runs three to four treatments a day, which is why management describes machines as only ~10% of renal revenue and consumables as the rest.

The unit of demand is the session. Frost & Sullivan (Nov-2025, via the NephroPlus prospectus) puts India's in-clinic treatments at 33.9mn in 2024, rising to 66.1mn by 2029 (14.3% CAGR, our calculation), for 0.28mn patients. In-clinic haemodialysis is more than 98% of all dialysis in India, and only 50% of patients are dialysed three times a week, the standard requirement. At a single-use pair per session, 2024 treatments imply ~67.8mn fistula needles a year (our calculation).

Dialyser demand is sessions divided by reuse, and reuse is the swing factor.

  • The MD described reuse ranging from single use to ten times in Mumbai centres, 7 to 8 on average and a single-use share of only 20-25% (both Aug-2023). The industry benchmark is a Fresenius dialyser reused seven times.
  • By Oct-2024 he put single use at 30-35% of the market (from 10% when Polymed entered), heading to 50% in two to three years. That would raise dialyser consumption without a single extra session.
  • Against that, the largest chain, NephroPlus, reprocesses dialysers for the same patient with its own patented Renova system, and CDSCO says it has received no complaints about reuse of single-use devices in five years.
  • Management sized Indian consumption at 8-12mn dialysers a year (Feb-2023).

Polymed's portfolio and position

Polymed makes every item in the session except concentrates, under its own brands:

  • Dial-Ezee haemodialysis machine: touchscreen, bicarbonate dialysis with ultrafiltration and sodium profiling, inbuilt SLED, variable dialysate flow of 100 to 800 ml/min, single or dual blood pump, and an optional Polytox endotoxin filter that fits only Dial-Ezee.
  • Haem-O-Flux dialyser: PES membrane, mid and high flux, three surface-area variants, shelf life 36 months.
  • Haem-O-Line bloodlines (pre- and post-pump) with a shelf life of 60 months, and transducer protectors.
  • AV fistula needles in 15G, 16G and 17G, plus the Poly Protekt safety fistula needle.
  • Haemocent and Haemocent Pro double- and triple-lumen haemodialysis catheter kits, also sold as components.
  • Peritoneal dialysis set and Y-shaped PD transfusion set, for acute rather than home PD.

How the line was built.

  • Fistula needles, bloodlines, HD catheters and PD sets were already in the FY16 range. The company dates its renal entry to 2018 and launched its dialyser in FY19; it describes itself as the first Indian company to make dialysers indigenously, a claim CRISIL repeats.
  • The machine came in two steps. Polymed first resold an imported machine from Jul-2020, and haemodialysis machines appear among traded goods in the placement document; the whole traded line, which also held blood-collection tubes, was only Rs. 88mn in FY22.
  • Its own machine came out of clinical trials with assembly starting in Aug-2022. Local content rose from ~35% to 50%+ by Feb-2024, with circuit boards moved from Taiwanese and Korean suppliers to Bangalore, and the MD says it was the first dialysis machine to receive BIS certification.
  • In the pipeline: an HDF (haemodiafiltration) machine made in India, targeted for mid-to-end 2026, 7-8 further renal products, and a pain-reducing needle technology.

Capacity and service.

  • Management planned in Jul-2021 to raise renal capacity to three times its Apr-2021 level by Mar-2022; by May-2022 it described dialysis capacity at the IMT Faridabad plant as "almost double". A further "tripling" was planned from FY25, after Polymed said in Oct-2024 that it was "short of products right now". Capacity was to "almost double" again by end-FY26.
  • The PLI project at Faridabad is shown as commissioned, with Rs. 556mn invested against Rs. 720mn committed. A separate government grant of Rs. 100mn supports a Rs. 1.0bn project for polymer tubing components, including bloodline and dialysis accessories.
  • Service is the differentiator management stresses: 30+ engineers, each able to cover 20-30 machines, and a three-year warranty followed by service contracts. Management says "we don't have any significant service revenue".

Revenue: nineteen-fold in eight years, then a stall

Renal revenue rose from Rs. 99mn in FY18 to Rs. 1.9bn in FY26, 19x on our calculation, lifting its share of the product table from 1.9% to 10% of consolidated revenue. Growth was uneven:

  • FY23-FY24: 27% and 25% on the placement-document figures (our calculation), against management's descriptions of around 40% for FY23 and 21-22% for FY24.
  • FY25: 60% (our calculation; the company's standalone figure is 60.1%). Management credited mandatory licensing of devices from Oct-2023, which it said ended unregulated parallel imports, and a rise in dialysis reimbursement from Rs. 1,200 to Rs. 1,800 per session that drew more standalone centres.
  • FY26: +24.5% as reported, which the MD described on the call as growth of "close to around 20%". Management gave three reasons:
    1. distributor inventory realignment around the Sep-2025 GST cut (2QFY26);
    2. pressure from Chinese suppliers (3QFY26);
    3. government contracts delayed by budgeting.
  • 1QFY27: revenue fell 3.8% to Rs. 432mn because Polymed "made a conscious choice to raise prices to push the raw material price increase rather than defend volume at any cost".

The quarterly run-rate shows where the growth went. Revenue held between Rs. 442mn and Rs. 449mn in each of the first three quarters of FY26, jumped 25.4% qoq in 4QFY26 (our calculation) and fell back to Rs. 432mn in 1QFY27. The four FY26 quarters as last reported sum to Rs. 1,901mn (our calculation), slightly above the annual Rs. 1,876mn, because the 1QFY27 deck restated 1QFY26 from Rs. 435mn to Rs. 449mn.

The guidance record. Renal has been the least reliable line in Polymed's guidance (Section A3). Revenue targets were cut in four of the five years from FY23 to FY27, and machine targets were cut in every year from FY24 to FY26. The one beat, FY25, followed the start of device licensing.

Machines and the installed base: razor and blades, but not locked in

The machine is the entry ticket to a dialysis unit's consumables, but Polymed's model differs from the MNCs'.

  • The MNC model. Fresenius and Nipro "place machines in the hospital" and then supply consumables under contracts of 5 to 7 years, which their balance sheets allow.
  • Polymed's model. Polymed sells the machine outright, for ~Rs. 0.5-0.6mn per unit (May-2023), priced 5-7% below MNC machines, and relies on service and local content to win the consumables.

Polymed's ~1,100 machines are only 2.8% of India's ~40,000 (our calculation on F&S's installed base). Management's ~10% share claim therefore implies that most of its dialysers, bloodlines and needles go into other brands' machines. Dialysers and needles are not machine-specific, so we read the installed base as a door-opener rather than a lock-in.

The scale gap is wide. Fresenius and Nipro have "installations of over 40,000-50,000 machines" in India, and the MD has put Fresenius alone at 70%-80% of India's installed base. NephroPlus alone ran 5,562 machines in Sep-2025, having added 1,900 since Mar-2023 (our calculation), more than Polymed's entire installed base.

The FY26 shortfall against the machine target came from tenders. Management said that "certain government contracts are delayed", that it had bid for 600+ machines, and that its current visibility for FY26 was 450 machines ("it can be 470 also"), with the rest likely to slip into FY27 "because of budgeting issues".

Market: India's dialysis is growing fast, but through public programmes and price-driven chains

Demand and capacity (Frost & Sullivan via the NephroPlus prospectus; vendor-commissioned, Nov-2025):

  • India had 4.2mn diagnosed ESRD patients in 2024, but only 0.28mn were on dialysis. That is forecast to reach 0.52mn by 2029, a 12.7% CAGR.
  • It has ~5,000 dialysis clinics, more than 80% of them private, and ~40,000 machines against a stated need of more than 105,000.
  • The organized chains hold ~20% of a services market of $818.0mn (2024) that F&S expects to reach $2.0bn by 2029.

The MD's own sizing was ~Rs. 10.0bn in May-2021, 95% of it imported. By May-2023 he put the market at Rs. 10-11bn, of which consumables were Rs. 8-9bn and machines Rs. 2.0-2.5bn, on 3,000-4,000 machine sales a year. He put industry growth at ~25% in May-2025.

Public money sets the price. Management estimates that 70-80% of patients are treated in government-funded centres, and the programmes pay per session:

  • PMNDP. The Pradhan Mantri National Dialysis Programme, launched in 2016, runs 1,925 centres with 13,824 haemodialysis machines in 751 districts (dashboard, Sep-2026). Those machines are 35% of F&S's national installed base (our calculation; different dates). Annual sessions rose from 2.5mn to 7.0mn in five years.
  • PPP model. Where PMNDP runs as a PPP (in 14 states and UTs), the private partner supplies the machines, water plant, dialysers and consumables. Contracts run 5 years (extendable), at a bid price that escalates 3% a year.
  • PM-JAY. The insurer pays Rs. 1,500 per haemodialysis session in tier-3 cities and Rs. 1,800 in tier-1 and tier-2 (HBP 2022); F&S puts the average at Rs. 1,650.

Polymed's consumables are therefore bought mainly by PPP operators and chains that bid for low per-session rates.

Private chains are consolidating the buying. NephroPlus is the largest. It served 29,281 patients in FY25, ~10% of India's dialysis patients, from 468 clinics (176 of them under PPP). DCDC runs more than 200 PPP clinics; Apex Kidney Care, Apollo Dialysis, RAHI Care, VitusCare and 7Med follow.

The chains buy centrally and are integrating backwards:

  • NephroPlus sources the dialysers, bloodlines and fistula needles for its Indian clinics within India.
  • It owns proprietary bloodline moulds and has contract manufacturers assemble the sets.
  • It reprocesses dialysers.

Its whole materials bill, across India, the Philippines and Uzbekistan, was Rs. 1.9bn in FY25, about the size of Polymed's renal revenue.

Polymed counts NephroPlus and DCDC among its customers, which makes the chains both the growth channel and the main source of price pressure.

Global context. Fresenius Medical Care's 20-F (CY25) gives the frame:

  • 4.5mn patients worldwide, growing 4-5% a year, 89% of them on in-centre haemodialysis;
  • a global dialysis market of €81-85bn, of which €16bn is products;
  • ~450mn dialysers sold a year, 38% of them made by Fresenius;
  • 110,000 haemodialysis machines installed in 2025, ~50% of them by Fresenius.

Vendor estimates put the global dialyser market at $4.0bn (IMARC, 2025) and haemodialysis catheters at $1.11bn (360iResearch, 2026).

Competition: MNCs above, Chinese imports below, and no Indian peer

Polymed positions itself between the two tiers on price. Its machines sell 5-7% below MNC machines, and Chinese consumables were ~15% cheaper than Polymed's in May-2023. Against both, it offers local service and training ("Chinese don't offer any service"), local content for public buyers, and a full consumables basket including a safety fistula needle.

Under public procurement rules, a Class-I local supplier needs at least 50% local content. The machine's ~50% local content is at that threshold. A Department of Pharmaceuticals order of Oct-2024 that deems PLI makers Class-II suppliers lists Nipro for dialysers but not Polymed, although a maker can still qualify as Class-I on its own content.

Regulation and policy: duty inversion, a trade-remedy case and a PLI that has not paid

Tariffs and the inverted duty structure.

  • Dialysers were duty-free until the Feb-2022 Budget imposed 7.5%. Management says Chinese makers then shifted to bases in ASEAN countries and relabelled product there to enter India at zero duty under the ASEAN FTA.
  • In May-2026 the MD put Chinese imports at "0% duty, only 5% GST" on the finished product. Polymed, by contrast, pays 2.5% customs duty on imported raw materials and 18% GST on inputs.
  • The FY26 annual report names renal as the segment hit by this duty inversion and by "dumping from China".

Imports under the dialysis tariff line (HS 90189031, which also covers machines) show the route shifting:

  • China. Declared quantities fell 39% in FY26 to 10.6mn units (our calculation).
  • Malaysia and Hong Kong. Malaysia rose 38% to 6.1mn units, and Hong Kong jumped to 2.5mn units.
  • By value. China's share of the line fell from 35% in FY23 to 21% in FY26, while Malaysia and Hong Kong reached 18%. The line's total value grew 37% over FY23-FY26 (our calculation).

We read this as consistent with management's account of Chinese product being rerouted, not as proof of it.

The anti-dumping case. The Directorate General of Trade Remedies opened Case AD (OI) (17/2026) on 25 Jun 2026 on Polymed's application.

  • Scope. The case covers disposable dialysers from China and Malaysia, with product types defined by surface area and flux; housing material was added in Aug-2026. Named Fresenius brands are excluded, which we read as targeting the low-priced product rather than the premium MNC lines.
  • Periods. The period of investigation is CY25, and the injury period runs from FY23.
  • Standing. Polymed is "the major domestic producer". The only other producer, Nipro Medical India, is a 100% export-oriented unit.
  • The injury claim, as summarized by DGTR. Subject imports rose "significantly in absolute terms", with price undercutting and depression, and the domestic industry "has incurred financial losses, cash losses and negative return on capital employed during the period of investigation". This is the first disclosure of the economics of Polymed's dialyser business.
  • Status. 11 parties registered, including five Chinese producers, a Malaysian producer and two importers. Questionnaire responses were due by 9 Sep 2026, and no preliminary findings had been published by 24 Sep 2026.
  • Management's view. Management has claimed an injury margin of 20% and expects "something will happen between now and end of the year". It has also raised the issue with the Health Minister.

PLI: approved, invested, unpaid.

  • Approval. Polymed is a Category A applicant under Target Segment 3 (anaesthetics, cardio-respiratory and renal-care devices) for seven products made at Faridabad: dialyser, machine, PD kit, fistula needle, bloodline, haemodialysis catheter and transducer protector.
  • Terms. The scheme pays 5% on incremental sales over FY20. A Category A applicant must show a minimum of incremental sales that rises each year, to Rs. 2.8bn in FY27, and the maximum incentive is Rs. 1.2bn.
  • Why it has missed. On our proxy, renal revenue less the FY20 base, incremental sales fell short of the threshold in every year from FY23 to FY26, and the gap widened (Exhibit 43).
  • Outcome. As of Jul-2026 Polymed had received no incentive, while Nipro had received Rs. 349mn. The MD had expected Rs. 400-450mn in 2021; by 2025 he answered "Zero" and said he was "building the business not on based on PLI".

Device regulation and reimbursement.

  • Dialysis machines are Class C devices under the Medical Devices Rules 2017. Licensing of Class C/D devices began in Oct-2023, which management credits for ending unregulated parallel imports.
  • The HDF machine faces a reimbursement ceiling: PM-JAY pays a flat per-session rate, so management expects HDF only in private centres, ~30-40% of the market.
  • The Parliamentary Standing Committee's CKD report (its 177th, Aug-2026) recommends promoting peritoneal dialysis as "the preferred first-line renal replacement therapy for clinically suitable patients". We would watch this, because Polymed's PD range is acute-care only.

Company-specific growth drivers, ranked

  1. Consumables pull-through from a larger installed base (management target; timing FY27-FY28).
    • Management plans a 20% rise in machine sales in FY27 and has a tender pipeline of 600+ machines.
    • It has hired a renal head from Nipro, Fresenius and DaVita to work the chains and distributors.
    • Our view: the lever is conversion of PPP and chain tenders, where lumpy timing produced the FY26 miss.
  2. A trade-remedy duty on Chinese and Malaysian dialysers (event-driven; management expects a decision by end-2026).
    • A duty would let Polymed recover the volume it gave up in 1QFY27 without cutting price.
    • Our view: the outcome is binary for FY27 renal growth, and the exclusion of Fresenius brands limits the benefit to the low-price segment.
  3. Session growth and public funding (structural). F&S expects treatments to grow at a 14.3% CAGR, and PMNDP sessions have nearly tripled in five years. Every session consumes Polymed's needles and bloodlines whichever machine it runs on.
  4. A shift to single-use dialysers (management expectation). Moving from 30-35% to 50% single use would lift dialyser volume per session. Our view: chains' reprocessing economics and the absence of any reuse rule make this slow.
  5. New products (management target; 2026-2028). These are the HDF machine, 7-8 further renal products, and exports of renal products once capacity allows. HDF is ~10% of Indian dialysis and "dominated by large multinationals like Fresenius and Nipro".

Company-specific margin drivers, ranked

  1. Price, set by imports. The DGTR summary of Polymed's own injury claim describes losses, cash losses and negative ROCE on dialysers in CY25. That conflicts with the MD's May-2025 statement that renal was "not a loss making … the operating margin is positive" with PAT expected by end-FY26. The two cover different scopes (dialysers only versus the whole renal line) and periods, and we do not reconcile them. Management chose price over volume in 1QFY27.
  2. Duty inversion on inputs. Import content has fallen below 50%, but the 2.5% customs duty and 18% GST on inputs weigh against duty-free imports.
  3. Utilization. On management's statements, renal capacity has been doubled or tripled more than once since FY22, while revenue has been flat for five quarters. We infer under-absorption of plant and of the fixed service network until volumes recover.
  4. Mix. Machines, ~10% of renal revenue, are sold outright with no significant service income, and management declines to give product margins.
  5. Incentives. PLI has paid nothing so far; its remaining upside is capped at Rs. 1.2bn over the scheme life and needs thresholds that renal has not met.

What to watch

Open questions for management are in the question bank: the renal product split, dialyser volumes and reuse assumptions, machine margins, PPP tender wins, the basis of the share claims, and the reconciliation of the DGTR injury claim with earlier profitability statements.
