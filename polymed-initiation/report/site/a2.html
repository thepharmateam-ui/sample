A2 How Polymed makes money

Polymed earns its money from:

  • Volume. It sold 960mn units devices in 9MFY25.
  • Low unit prices. Infusion devices sell at Rs. 10-20 each, by management's account.
  • A cost base it largely controls. The group makes most of what it sells in-house, from moulds and tooling to automated assembly lines and, since 2025, gamma sterilization. On our calculation from the standalone accounts, FY26 costs as a share of revenue were:
    • materials consumed 32.4%
    • employees 18.8%
    • job work 6.3%
    • power 3.4%
    • freight 2.0%

Standalone operating EBITDA margin was 26.8%.

  • Exports: 64.2% of standalone revenue was earned in foreign exchange, and foreign-exchange outgo equalled 46% of those earnings, a partial natural hedge (our calculation).

We see Polymed's edge among Indian device makers in a combination of:

  1. scale in one high-volume device;
  2. in-house tooling and automation;
  3. a distributor network across 125+ countries.

The unit of demand: consumption, not capital spending

Almost everything Polymed sells is used once and discarded, so demand follows clinical activity rather than hospital budgets:

  • one IV cannula per insertion, re-sited every few days;
  • a pair of fistula needles and a bloodline set per haemodialysis session;
  • one blood bag per donation;
  • one feeding tube, urine bag or drain per patient episode.

The product glossary in the appendix sets out the unit of demand for each line.

This makes revenue recurring and less cyclical than equipment revenue. Its weaknesses are:

  • Low prices: most items sell for tens of rupees, not thousands.
  • Tenders: buyers can switch suppliers at tender time.
  • Channel inventory: stock held by distributors, which management blamed for much of the FY26 slowdown (Section A3).

The shift into cardiology and orthopaedics changes the unit economics: management says a single device there sells for thousands of rupees, sometimes above Rs. 20,000. We read that as fewer units, more clinical selling and a longer path through regulatory approval.

Price and volume: most growth has come from units

Management has occasionally split growth into price and volume:

  • FY22: the MD estimated that volume contributed about 75% of growth and price about 25%.
  • 9MFY25: devices sold grew 19% against consolidated revenue growth of 23.2%.

The placement document describes the pricing pattern for mature products: average selling prices decline over a product's life, 'partially offset' by volume and cost reductions. Raw-material and currency changes are passed on to customers 'at periodic intervals', which means with a lag.

In India, prices of notified devices that are not under a ceiling cannot rise more than 10% in any 12 months under Para 20 of DPCO 2013, and MRPs are subject to an active trade-margin debate (Section B3).

For FY27 management said it had already secured price increases of 3-5% against a ~20% rise in crude-linked raw-material costs, higher freight and Haryana's wage revision.

The value chain: Polymed makes most of what it sells

In-house tooling and automation are the least visible part of this chain. The placement document says moulds are fabricated on in-house CNC machines and that the robotics on the assembly lines is developed and programmed in-house; CRISIL attributes the operating margin partly to in-house tool design. Automation is also the lever management cites against the Haryana minimum-wage increase: 'we are pushing more on automation right now'.

The main outsourced stage is job work. 84% of it went to Vitromed Healthcare in FY24 and 84% in FY26 (our calculation). This is a promoter-owned partnership, discussed under governance.

Channel and customers: distributors abroad, a direct sales force at home

Exports. Exports from India go through distributors; the acquired subsidiaries also sell direct in Italy, the US and Mexico.

  • Network. Polymed had 260 overseas distributors at Jun-2024 (up from 215 in FY22). Many hold local or regional exclusivity 'subject to certain conditions', usually under agreements of 2 years.
  • Ordering. Distributors buy on purchase orders. The placement document notes that there is no significant order backlog and no long-term customer contract, and customers 'often undertake vendor rationalisation'.
  • Direct presence. Management's Vision 2030 plan is to go direct with new product ranges in 3-4 European markets and in Brazil. In Europe it is currently direct only in Italy, where Plan1Health and Citieffe are based.

India. Polymed runs its own field force alongside distributors.

  • Scale. At Jun-2024 it had 440 sales staff covering 8,000 hospitals and 506 distributors; the FY26 annual report cites 590+ direct-sales representatives across India, the US, Italy and Mexico.
  • Customers. Management names corporate chains (Apollo, Max, Fortis, Manipal, Aster DM), diagnostic laboratories (Thyrocare, Metropolis, Dr Lal PathLabs) and dialysis chains (NephroPlus, DCDC) among its customers.
  • Government business. It has been shrinking: down 10% in 1QFY26 and 14% in 2QFY26, while private-sector sales grew. Management describes this as a deliberate exit from government business that does not meet its margin thresholds.

Brand versus OEM.

  • Split. About 72% of FY22 revenue was sold under the PolyMed brand and 28% as OEM or private label. Exports ran at ~75% own brand, and management describes India as entirely branded.
  • Target. The stated aim in May-2022 was to get as close as possible to 80% own brand; management said white label cannot be avoided entirely.
  • Buyers. The OEM customers are not named. CRISIL's 2023 report says Polymed supplies 'other big players'.

Concentration is moderate.

  • Top ten customers: 27.6% of FY22 revenue, 29.0% in FY24 and 31.5% in 1QFY25.
  • Largest customer: between 4.9% and 7.3%.
  • Threshold: no customer has reached the single-customer disclosure threshold in any year since FY19.

The top customers are unnamed; given the distributor model, we infer that most are distributors. The placement document's own risk factors nonetheless describe the business as 'concentrated among a few significant customers'.

Costs and margins: materials, people, job work, power and freight

How to read the cost table

  • Calculation. All ratios are our calculations from the standalone financial statements, each cited in its tooltip.
  • Operating EBITDA. For FY13-FY24 we deduct other income from the EBITDA printed in the directors' report. FY25 and FY26 use the company's operating EBITDA, which already excludes it.
  • Materials. 'Materials consumed' excludes purchases of traded goods and inventory changes, so it runs below the raw-material share the company quotes. The placement document gives 38.2%, 38.0% and 33.8% of total revenue for FY22-FY24.
  • R&D. Our ratio divides R&D expense by standalone revenue; the annual report's 1.6%, used elsewhere in this report, divides the same spend by consolidated revenue.

We draw four patterns from the table.

  1. Materials set the cycle.
    • The materials ratio jumped to 37.7% in FY22 and operating margin fell to 24.1%. Management blamed raw-material and freight inflation and cut its FY22 EBITDA guidance twice.
    • It recovered to 33.1% by FY24.
    • Two features make Polymed exposed: 71.8% of raw materials were imported in FY24, and the company holds ~2.5-3 months of raw-material cover. We infer that a crude spike reaches the P&L only once that cover is used up; management said the cover limited the impact in early FY27, while it negotiated price increases.
  2. Employee cost is the next pressure point. At 18.8% of FY26 revenue it is Polymed's second-largest cost. Haryana's minimum wage rose 35% from 1-Apr-2026, and standalone employee cost rose 29% yoy in 1QFY27. Management's answer is more automation.
  3. Job work has fallen steadily from 9.0% to 6.3% of revenue. We read this as in-house automation taking over labour-intensive steps, and as the clearest evidence in the accounts of the automation strategy at work.
  4. Freight is now a visible cost. It ran at 0.6-0.9% of standalone revenue over FY19-FY24, then rose to 1.5% in FY25 and 2.0% in FY26 (our calculation). Management says logistics costs have risen 2-3 times in the last six months.

Currency. Polymed's exposure is mainly to the US dollar and the euro. The placement document notes that a weaker rupee also raises the cost of imported raw materials and of borrowings, which are 'primarily U.S. Dollar denominated'; with FX earnings well above FX outgo, we infer that a weaker rupee helps on net.

  • Unhedged exposure. At 30-Jun-2024, unhedged foreign-currency exposure was Rs. 1.6bn against forward contracts of Rs. 639mn.
  • No forward cover at FY26 year-end. At 31-Mar-2026 no forward contracts were outstanding, against a net unhedged US-dollar receivable of Rs. 2.4bn. FX gains of Rs. 381mn were booked in other income in FY26, below operating EBITDA; we read this as meaning the reported operating margin understates the full currency benefit.
  • Management's view. It cited rupee depreciation as a partial offset to FY27 cost inflation, since 60-70% of the business is international.

Export incentives. Consolidated export incentives were Rs. 133mn in FY26, below the Rs. 167mn the standalone company booked in FY19 under the earlier MEIS scheme. Management put the cost of MEIS's withdrawal at Rs. 70-80mn in FY21 and called the replacement RoDTEP rate 'very, very low'.

Working capital: a long cycle by design

Exporting through distributors across 125+ countries ties up capital.

  • Receivables. Days fell from 80 days in FY22 to 73 days in FY24.
  • Inventory cover. Raw-material cover is ~2.5-3 months.
  • Cash conversion cycle. On the definition in the company's presentation, the standalone cycle lengthened from 183 days in FY25 to 215 days in FY26. Management quotes 140 days on its own measure.

Management attributes the FY26 rise in receivables to 'better terms' extended to international customers to drive growth and retain them, and said it expects the receivable cycle to stay 'similar' in FY27. We treat receivable days as a leading indicator of distributor stress: a further rise alongside weak export growth would point to channel stuffing rather than a demand recovery.
