B3 India medtech: market, policy and demand

India is Polymed's fastest-growing region and 31% of its FY26 revenue (Section A1). It is also where policy moves fastest, and in two directions at once. Industrial policy protects and subsidizes local makers through production incentives, local-content rules in public tenders and trade remedies. Price policy pushes the other way, through the cap on MRP increases, package rates for insured care and a live debate on capping trade margins. This section sets out the market, the policy on both sides and the demand drivers.

How big is the market? The estimates do not reconcile

No two sources size the Indian device market on the same basis. They differ on whether exports, capital equipment and diagnostics are included, on the year, and on who paid for the study. We show them side by side rather than choose one. Estimates from reports commissioned for offer documents or published by market-research firms are vendor estimates.

For Polymed the relevant pool is consumables and disposables, which the sources also size differently:

  • CRISIL puts consumables and disposables at Rs. 195.0bn in FY24, after 13% annual growth over FY19-FY24, and expects 11-12% a year to Rs. 290-300bn by FY28 (vendor estimate). It put Polymed's share at 1.9% in FY23, on domestic revenue of Rs. 3.4bn.
  • Frost & Sullivan puts disposables and consumables at $4.3bn in 2023, 26% of the device market, rising to $17.1bn by 2030 (vendor estimate).
  • 1Lattice puts surgical disposables alone at $3.9bn in FY25, rising to $6.4bn by FY30 (vendor estimate).

We use CRISIL's consumables figure as the working estimate of Polymed's domestic pool, because it is the only one that also measures Polymed's share. On that basis Polymed is a small player in a large market, and management frames the opportunity the same way: in Feb-2025 the MD said Polymed had only covered one-third of India's hospitals, with a share of wallet below 20% where it is present, and in Nov-2025 he put that share at 5-7%.

Imports, exports and the substitution runway

Import dependence is just as contested:

Two readings survive the noise. First, consumables are less import-dependent than equipment: BCG puts imported equipment at 60-65% in 2025 against 50-55% for consumables, CRISIL puts consumables at 30-40% against ~80% for all devices, and CRISIL says imports are lower in needles and syringes and higher in catheters and cannulas. Second, on BCG's estimates the import share is falling, and BCG expects domestic production to meet 75% of demand by 2035 (consultancy projection). CRISIL puts China at 16% of India's imports of medical and scientific instruments in FY24.

India is also a sizeable exporter, and consumables are its largest export category:

  • Exports were $4.0bn in FY25 by the Department of Pharmaceuticals' count, of which consumables and disposables $1.9bn. The export council's figures are close but not identical ($4.0bn and $1.8bn).
  • Apr-Feb FY26: exports were flat at $3.7bn, with exports to the US down to $670.39mn in the tariff period, while imports were $9.0bn, of which $1.4bn from China.
  • Net position. F&S, citing IBEF, says India became a net exporter of consumables and disposables in 2022-23, and the government says net device imports fell for the first time in 2023.

Policy, part one: rules that protect and support local makers

Licensing. All devices have been regulated under the Medical Devices Rules 2017 since Apr-2020, with licensing phased in from Oct-2022 (Class A and B) and Oct-2023 (Class C and D). Infusion sets are Class B and haemodialysers Class C. India had 4,108 licensed device manufacturers in Mar-2026. A draft amendment of Jun-2026 would make a notified-body audit of the quality system a condition of a licence and shorten review times. Management welcomed licensing as a way to push out unlicensed makers; in Aug-2022 the MD put the unregulated share of the industry at 70-75% before the rules took effect.

The draft devices law. The draft Drugs, Medical Devices and Cosmetics Bill 2026 has 190 sections and would replace the 1940 Act, add a technical advisory board for devices and extend criminal penalties for substandard or misbranded products to devices. A coalition of 11 industry associations led by AiMeD wrote to the Health Minister on 11-Aug-2026 asking for a separate Medical Devices Act and an autonomous regulator, and objecting to jail terms of 1-7 years. These details come from press reports; the official text was not available to us.

Production incentives (PLI). The scheme pays 5% of incremental sales over an FY20 base, subject to minimum sales growth. For Category A applicants the FY27 threshold is Rs. 2.8bn of incremental sales, and the incentive is capped at Rs. 1.2bn per applicant and target segment over FY23-FY27.

  • Polymed. It is approved for seven renal products (dialysers, dialysis machines, PD kits, fistula needles, bloodlines, haemodialysis catheters and transducer protectors) at Faridabad. The government lists the project as commissioned, with committed investment of Rs. 720mn and actual investment of Rs. 556mn. Polymed had received no incentive up to FY25: it is not among the recipients in the Jul-2026 parliamentary answer, and asked in May-2025 whether it had realized anything for renal, the MD said "No, Zero". He has said the scheme "has not worked well" because the thresholds are the same for equipment, implants and consumables. Whether anything was claimed for FY26 is [not disclosed].
  • Who has been paid. Incentives up to FY25 went to seven companies, including Nipro India Corporation, whose Satara plant makes dialysers, which received Rs. 349mn. The two official totals conflict: Rs. 2.7bn to FY25 in a Jul-2026 parliamentary answer, against Rs. 1.6bn to Dec-2025 in a Mar-2026 release.
  • A second scheme. Polymed also has a project under the scheme to reduce import dependence (MIS-RID) for polymer tubing, bloodline and central venous catheter components: project cost Rs. 1.0bn, grant Rs. 100mn.

Public procurement. Government buyers must prefer local suppliers. A Class-I local supplier needs at least 50% local content, and 19 device lines, including syringes, hollow needles, urine catheters, cannulas and blood-transfusion apparatus, are open only to Class-I suppliers whatever the tender value. Poly Medicure appears on the illustrative supplier list for these lines. Global tenders below a value threshold are barred unless a device is on an exemption list, which covered 364 devices to Mar-2024; our search of the published lists found none of Polymed's core products on it. Since Oct-2024 PLI makers count as Class-II suppliers; Nipro is listed for dialysers and Polymed is not.

Taxes and duties. GST on devices was cut from 12% to 5% on 22-Sep-2025, and management linked a one-off distributor destocking in 2QFY26 to the change (Section A3). Imports of medical instruments and equipment (HS 9018 to HS 9022) carry a health cess of 5% on top of basic duty. The duty structure is inverted: CRISIL notes GST of 5-18% across devices, and Polymed says inputs and job work attract higher GST and customs duty than finished devices, which favours importers in renal. From 2-Apr-2026 the government removed basic customs duty on a list of petrochemicals and polymers, including PP, PVC and polycarbonate, "in view of ongoing conflict in West Asia"; the relief lapsed after 15-Jul-2026.

The dialyser anti-dumping case

On 25-Jun-2026 the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) opened an anti-dumping investigation into disposable haemodialysis dialysers from China and Malaysia, on Polymed's petition. The case, the policy measure most directly aimed at Polymed's own problem, is set out below; Section A4.3 covers its effect on the renal business.

  • Scope. The period of investigation is calendar 2025, and the injury period also covers FY23-FY25. Certain Fresenius dialyser brands are excluded.
  • Standing. Polymed told DGTR that it is "the major domestic producer"; the only other one is Nipro Medical India, a 100% export-oriented unit that makes dialysers almost entirely for export. DGTR accepted that Polymed constitutes the domestic industry.
  • Injury claimed. DGTR's initiation notice records prima facie evidence that dumped imports rose "significantly in absolute terms", undercut and depressed prices, and left the domestic industry with "financial losses, cash losses and negative return on capital employed" in the investigation period. Because Polymed alone constitutes the domestic industry in the case, we read this as a statement that its dialyser business made losses in the investigation period; the company does not report profitability by therapy line. Management says it claimed an injury margin of 20%.
  • Process. Questionnaire responses were due by 9-Sep-2026, and 11 parties have registered, including Weigao's blood-purification arm, Jiangxi Sanxin and a Malaysian exporter. No preliminary finding had been published by 24-Sep-2026. Management hopes for a decision "between now and end of the year".

Management says most Chinese makers ship to India at zero duty under the ASEAN free-trade agreement, and in May-2023 the MD described Chinese product being relabelled in Malaysia or Thailand and sent on to India duty-free. We read the trade data as consistent with that account: imports under the dialyser tariff line from Malaysia rose from $210k in FY23 to $13.74mn in FY26, and in units from 58,302 units to 6,060,880 units, while direct imports from China fell to 10,592,925 units in FY26 from 17,298,455 units a year earlier. The line also covers machines, so the data cannot prove the route.

Policy, part two: the price of a device and the trade-margin debate

Section B1 explains how the MRP and the trade margin work. Here we set out where the policy stands.

Today's rules. Non-scheduled devices, which include all of Polymed's consumables, face only the Para 20 limit: their MRP may rise by at most 10% in twelve months. Device prices have been set directly three times: ceiling prices on coronary stents and knee implants in 2017, and trade-margin caps on six devices in 2021, which the government says save consumers ~Rs. 10.0bn a year.

The Aug-Sep 2026 push. Three steps in six weeks turned trade margins on consumables into a live issue:

  1. Parliament, 7-Aug-2026. The standing committee on health recommended that "the gap should not be more than 20% between landing price and MRP for medical devices and drugs", and called for centralized, tender-based procurement of high-end devices and consumables by private empanelled hospitals.
  2. Maharashtra, 15-16 Sep 2026. The state drug regulator's price-monitoring unit surveyed inpatient consumables and found mark-ups of up to 2,841%, including on a Polymed IV set, and its commissioner asked the Department of Pharmaceuticals and NPPA to cap trade margins, issue guidelines on permissible price differentials and bring the items under DPCO. The drug regulators of Punjab, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu sent similar inputs.
  3. The Health Minister, 18-Sep-2026. After the department met private hospitals and FICCI on 17-Sep, J.P. Nadda directed it and NPPA to review the trade-margin exercises of 2004, 2008 and 2019 and to consult hospitals and industry. No public deadline has been set.

What NPPA can do. Trade press reports that NPPA has no blanket power to impose a single margin cap across non-scheduled devices; a broad cap would need an amendment to DPCO, or trade-margin rationalization category by category, the route taken for anti-cancer drugs in 2019 and for six devices in 2021. Non-scheduled products are 82% of the pharmaceutical market by value.

Who is on which side. The Association of Healthcare Providers, which represents around 30,000 private hospitals, argues that MRPs are "fixed upstream by manufacturers and distributors", that hospitals do not charge above MRP and that most treatment runs on insurance package rates. The domestic manufacturers' association (AiMeD) backs trade-margin caps based on ex-factory or landed import prices.

We see three outcomes, in rising order of impact on Polymed:

  1. No action, or monitoring only. The status quo, with the 10% limit on annual MRP increases.
  2. Trade-margin caps on named inpatient consumables (IV sets, syringes, catheters), as in 2021. MRPs of the affected products would fall towards a fixed mark-up over the price to distributor. On the 2021 precedent the cap applies to the channel, and the MD argued then that it "does not impact the manufacturers"; the risk to Polymed is a second-round push by hospitals and distributors for lower purchase prices.
  3. A general cap of 20% over landing price across devices, as the parliamentary committee proposed. That would require a DPCO amendment and would compress the channel's margin on every MRP-billed product.

In every case the exposure depends on how much of Polymed's domestic revenue is billed to patients at MRP rather than on package rates or institutional contracts, which the company does not disclose. We read the survey's single Polymed data point as a sign that it sets MRPs below rivals', not as proof.

Demand: what drives consumption

The demand story is well known; what matters for Polymed is how it is paid for. The shift from cash to insurance and government schemes moves hospitals from itemized MRP billing to fixed package rates, which rewards cheaper, reliable consumables; management says the shift, together with dearer imports, is pushing hospitals towards domestic products. Dialysis is the clearest case: the government pays a fixed rate per session, private operators run many public centres, and every session uses a dialyser, a bloodline set and a pair of fistula needles (Section A4.3).

We read India as Polymed's most attractive market on volume and its most uncertain on price. The volume case rests on insurance, PM-JAY and PMNDP; the price risk rests on a trade-margin decision for which no deadline has been set. We would watch the Department of Pharmaceuticals' assessment, the DGTR's preliminary findings on dialysers and any NPPA notification on inpatient consumables.
