A4.6 Anaesthesia, respiratory and critical care

This chapter covers two different businesses under one heading.

  • Anaesthesia and respiratory is an old, low-priced range of airways, circuits, masks, suction catheters and spinal needles that Polymed has sold for two decades. It was last disclosed at 2.9% of standalone turnover.
  • Critical care is a sales division set up in recent years. It bundles ICU devices from several product families (central lines, arterial catheters, midlines, vial-access spikes, Huber needles) and is one of management's two domestic growth bets alongside cardiology.

Neither has a revenue line in the financial statements or the investor decks.

What the range includes and how it is used

Two points on how the range is used.

  1. Demand follows surgery and ventilation. Surgery drives airways, circuits, HME filters and spinal needles. Ventilator-days drive suction catheters and circuits. Respiratory epidemics drive masks and nasal cannulas. In the second COVID wave (1QFY22) Polymed raised respiratory capacity "three to four times" within weeks and sold an oxygen Y-connector at Rs. 10.
  2. Critical care is a customer segment, not a product line. Its catalogue page regroups items also sold under infusion, vascular access and gastroenterology. Their revenue is therefore split across the BRSR buckets, and a separate "critical care" number would overlap with A4.1 and A4.2.

What is disclosed, and what is not

The FY26 BRSR drops the anaesthesia and respiratory line. Its FY25 share (2.9%) is close to the rise in the infusion share, from 67.1% to 69.9%, which suggests the line was folded into infusion. That is our inference; the company has not said so. The bases also differ: the placement-document series is consolidated product sales, the BRSR is standalone turnover. The two show a business that has grown roughly in line with the group rather than faster than it.

Critical care: the growth project

Management's account of critical care has moved:

  • Launch date. The FY26 annual report dates entry to 2019. The placement document says the critical care division was launched in FY23, and on the May-2025 call the MD described the division as having "just started last year".
  • Scale claims. By Nov-2025 Polymed had launched 20 plus critical-care products in a year and was present in 500 hospitals, including top-tier chains. 6 new products were launched across infusion and critical care in 1QFY27.
  • Targets. In Aug-2023 management aimed for Rs. 0.75-1.0bn of critical-care revenue in three to four years, saying the segment "definitely has a better margin". In May-2025 it hoped the segment would grow "almost 2 and 1/2 times" in FY26. Neither outcome has been disclosed.
  • Investment. Polymed added 70 people across cardiology and critical care in FY25, whose "contribution is very less" so far, in the MD's words.

Why management likes the segment. It argues that critical care has few global competitors ("Cardiology ... 20 companies ... Critical Care is only 4-5 companies"). Indian demand is largely imported (80-90%, Oct-2024), and ICU patients consume many devices each: management put ICU beds at ~20% of hospital beds (Jul-2021). The segment is also the channel for oncology products (Section A4.2).

We read critical care as a cross-selling engine for devices Polymed already makes, sold by clinically trained staff, rather than a new product category. Its success should therefore show up in the domestic "others" line and in private-hospital growth, not in a separately reported number.

Market and competition

Market data are thin and vendor-based. Research and Markets pages put the global endotracheal-tube market at $2.54bn in 2026 and anaesthesia face masks at $252.42mn in 2025 (vendor estimates).

The multinationals are consolidating, and Chinese makers are widening their ranges:

  • Teleflex agreed in Dec-2025 to sell its Acute Care and Interventional Urology businesses to Intersurgical for $530mn, alongside a separate $1.5bn sale of its OEM unit to Montagu and Kohlberg. Acute Care covers airway management, anaesthesia, respiratory, humidification, oxygen therapy and regional anaesthesia. Teleflex had already sold respiratory lines to Medline in 2021.
  • ICU Medical, which bought Smiths Medical (Portex airway and tracheostomy products), reported Vital Care revenue of $437.9mn in CY25. That covers anaesthesia, respiratory, haemodynamic monitoring and regional anaesthesia, after most IV solutions moved to a joint venture. It names Intersurgical among its main Vital Care competitors.
  • BD and B. Braun are strong in regional anaesthesia: BD sells spinal and epidural needles and trays in its Medication Delivery Solutions unit, and B. Braun cites regional anaesthesia as a growth driver of its Hospital Care division.
  • BD in critical care. In Sep-2024 BD bought Edwards Lifesciences' Critical Care group for $3.9bn and renamed it Advanced Patient Monitoring. Its range includes arterial pressure monitoring products and pulmonary artery catheters, so the largest infusion competitor now also sells the monitoring systems that Polymed's arterial cannulas and catheters connect to.
  • Chinese makers are registering the same basic items in Europe: KDL completed CE registration for 22 products in CY25, including suction, feeding and urinary catheters.

In India, management says imports supply most of critical-care demand. CRISIL notes that Indian makers mostly produce basic items such as nasal oxygen cannulas, oxygen masks, ventilator circuits and mucus extractors, and that multinationals and imports dominate the consumables market, although the same report puts imports at only 30-40% of consumables sales.

Two domestic rivals show the competition in that basic range. Romsons sells anaesthesia, respiratory, urology and transfusion disposables; 1Lattice puts its FY24 revenue at Rs. 8.6bn and its EBITDA margin at 26.8%, and Temasek took a minority stake in it in Sep-2026. Angiplast makes nasal oxygen cannulas and oxygen and nebulizer masks and says it exports more than 80% of its output.

Polymed's position in the basic range rests on price and breadth within the same distributor basket as infusion. In critical care it rests on local manufacture against imports and on clinical selling. We see no evidence of a share lead in any single product here.

Regulation specific to the line

  • US. Polymed's FDA device listings include Class I respiratory and airway items that need no 510(k): tracheobronchial suction catheters and a suction catheter kit (Jaipur), nasal oxygen cannula, breathing circuit, oropharyngeal airway, and oxygen and venturi masks (Faridabad). These can be sold in the US without clearance. The suction catheter kit is listed under the proprietary name "McKesson", a US distributor, which points to private-label supply (Section A4.10).
  • India pricing. In 2014 the NPPA alleged that Polymed charged 20% above the maximum price on spinal needles. It demanded Rs. 9.3mn, of which Rs. 1.8mn was paid under protest. The contingent liability (Rs. 6.7mn) was shown in the FY25 accounts as since disposed of. The Maharashtra FDA survey reported in Sep-2026, which named a Polymed IV set, also found a Nebplus-brand nebuliser, a brand not in Polymed's catalogues, marked up 1,688%; the consultation on trade margins ordered that month would cover respiratory consumables too (Section A4.1).
  • Europe. Most of the range is low-risk under the MDR, but re-certification of each variant still costs time. Management said Polymed had to drop products during the MDR transition (Section A4.1).

Growth and margin drivers, ranked

  1. Critical-care cross-selling in Indian private hospitals (management target; our view: plausible but unmeasured). The levers are import substitution and clinical sales staff. It is measurable only through domestic growth.
  2. Surgical volumes and caesarean-heavy spinal-needle demand (our view). This is the base-load driver of the anaesthesia range.
  3. Export of Class I items to the US (our view: small). Polymed already lists them, and private label is one route.
  4. Margin (our view). Anaesthesia and respiratory items are commodity-like, and we read them as earning below the group average. Critical-care devices replace imports and management says they carry "a better margin". Neither margin is disclosed.

What to watch

Open questions for management (also in the question bank):

  • where the anaesthesia and respiratory line went in the FY26 BRSR;
  • critical-care revenue, and how it overlaps with vascular access;
  • whether the FY26 ambition for critical care to grow "almost 2 and 1/2 times" was met;
  • US private-label volumes of Class I respiratory items.
