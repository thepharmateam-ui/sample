A9 Management, governance and capital allocation

Polymed is a founder-run company. The Baid brothers built it and still run it, and the reported promoter group, which now includes two LLPs reclassified from the public category, holds 62.4% of the equity.

The strengths:

  • clean audit reports, with no auditor qualification in the five years to FY24;
  • a CRISIL rating of AA-/Positive;
  • a net cash balance sheet;
  • two QIPs that institutions absorbed.

Against these, we see five governance features that an investor should diligence before underwriting the FY30 plan:

  1. Job work with a promoter firm. Rs. 870mn of FY26 job work went to Vitromed Healthcare, a partnership of promoter-family members. That was 84% of all job-work charges (our calculation).
  2. A promoter group enlarged by reclassification. The reported holding moved from 44.7% to 62.4% after two LLPs that had been counted as public shareholders were reclassified. Neither step involved a purchase of shares.
  3. Promoter-family pay. It equalled 14.7% of standalone PAT in FY26 (our calculation), and the MD earned 497x the median employee.
  4. Slow deployment of the Aug-2024 QIP. Only 21% of the capex scheduled for deployment by FY26 was spent (our calculation), and the monitoring agency flagged that part of the idle money sat in hybrid mutual funds and a PMS.
  5. A mixed early record for the FY26 acquisitions. PendraCare contributed a pre-tax loss of Rs. 141mn in its first six months under Polymed, while Citieffe was close to breakeven after closing.

None of these is hidden. Each is disclosed in the annual report, the placement documents or the monitoring-agency reports. Institutions have not voted against the board in any number: no resolution at the 31st AGM drew more than 4.0% of institutional votes against, although none of the five items above was itself put to a vote.

Who runs Polymed

The operating company is still run by its founders:

  • Himanshu Baid (MD), who joined on 20-Sep-1996.
  • Rishi Baid (JMD), formerly with Miles Pharma in the US. Both describe themselves as first-generation entrepreneurs.
  • Their father, J.K. Baid, sits on the board as a non-executive director. Their mother, Mukulika Baid, stepped down from the board on 6-May-2025, the day Vishal Baid became an executive director.

The next generation is inside the business:

  • Vishal Baid is now an executive director, in charge of marketing, corporate finance and the Jaipur manufacturing operations.
  • Dhruv, Arham and Aaryaman Baid are employed in the company.

From 2025-26 a professional layer was added:

  • a CEO for India and APAC hired from Translumina, who earlier ran B. Braun in India;
  • a CEO for Brazil;
  • a head of the Indian renal business;
  • a President for strategy and corporate development.

The acquired businesses keep their own chief executives.

The board. At 31-Mar-2026 the board had 12 directors. Eight were non-executive, five of them independent, and one was an independent woman director.

  • Chairman. D.R. Mehta, a former SEBI chairman, has been on the board since 2005. He is now classified as a non-executive, non-independent director.
  • Re-appointment. Because he is older than 75 years, the 31st AGM re-appointed him by special resolution. 4.0% of institutional votes were cast against, the highest dissent of any resolution at the meeting.
  • Balance of the board. The four executive directors include three members of the promoter family. With the promoters' father and the Plan1 founder among the non-executives, the board has a slim independent balance by the standards of widely held companies. It still meets the one-third test that applies to a non-promoter non-executive chairman.

Shareholding: how the promoter group reached 62.4%

The promoter group's reported holding has swung between 44.7% and 66.0% over five years with almost no change in the family's own shareholdings. The swings are almost entirely the result of two reclassifications:

  • Zetta Matrix Consulting LLP. It held 9.4% in FY20 as a public shareholder and was moved into the promoter group in FY23.
  • Ezekiel Global Business Solutions LLP. It held 14.0% in FY20 and was listed as a major non-promoter shareholder through 31-Mar-2024. By 30-Jun-2024 it was inside the promoter group.

The arithmetic ties exactly:

  • The reported promoter holding at 31-Mar-2024 plus Ezekiel's then-public stake is 66.03% (our calculation). That is the promoter-group figure the placement document reported at 30-Jun-2024 (66.0%), weeks before the Aug-2024 QIP.
  • In FY20 the reported promoter group and the two LLPs together held 72.2%. In FY22 they held 66.2%. We attribute the fall between those years to dilution from the Feb-2021 QIP.

The documents we reviewed disclose neither who controls the two LLPs nor why they were reclassified. We therefore do not infer control. We do note three consequences:

  • The public float is 37.6%.
  • Public institutions held about 21.2% at the AGM record date (our calculation), with domestic mutual funds buying as foreign portfolio investors sold (Exhibit 117).
  • The effective free float is narrower than the pre-FY23 shareholding pattern implied.

Promoter shares carried no pledge at 30-Jun-2024.

Related parties: Vitromed and the family trust

Vitromed Healthcare is a Jaipur partnership of J.K. Baid, Rishi Baid, Vishal Baid and Polycure Martech Ltd, a company owned by directors and their relatives, according to the FY16 annual report; the FY26 report lists it among enterprises over which key management personnel and their relatives exercise significant influence.

  • Its role. It has been Polymed's main job-worker since at least FY09. It makes products and components from raw materials that Polymed supplies. It is not an OEM supplier.
  • Products. The Feb-2021 placement document named safety scalp-vein sets and silicone rubber sleeves among its products.
  • Two-way trade. Vitromed also buys goods from Polymed, so money flows in both directions.

Over time Vitromed's share of total job work has eased, but the rupee amount paid to it has grown (Exhibit 118).

In FY26 the job work paid to Vitromed equalled 5.2% of standalone revenue (our calculation).

The transactions are disclosed every year:

  • Shareholders set contract caps of Rs. 600mn, Rs. 750mn and Rs. 900mn for FY19-21.
  • In FY10 sales to Vitromed exceeded the then Section 297 approval limit by Rs. 8.8mn.
  • We found no related-party approval resolution in the 2025 or 2026 AGM notices. The FY26 annual report states that all related-party transactions were at arm's length.
  • All FY26 dealings with Vitromed together equalled 5.9% of consolidated revenue (our calculation). That is below the materiality threshold in the company's policy – the lower of Rs. 10.0bn or 10% of consolidated turnover – above which shareholder approval would be needed.

We see two questions for management, both of which bear on margins:

  1. Is the pricing demonstrably arm's-length? The disclosures give amounts, not the terms or the margin Vitromed earns.
  2. Why is the capacity not brought in-house? The QIP funded new plants but did not absorb Vitromed.

CSR and the family trust.

  • Polymed routes a large share of its CSR spend through the Jai Chand Lal Hulasi Devi Baid Charitable Trust, a promoter-family trust: Rs. 21mn in FY26, or 51% of CSR spend (our calculation).
  • The trust received Rs. 3.0mn in FY21 and Rs. 21mn in FY24.

Other related parties.

  • Egypt associate. Ultra for Medical Products paid Polymed a dividend and governing-council share of Rs. 33mn in FY26.
  • Rent. Rent to promoter company Jai Polypan is immaterial.
  • Plan1 founder. Alessandro Balboni received business-development fees of Rs. 28mn in FY24.

Remuneration

Pay for the two promoter managing directors has two parts:

  • A fixed element. From 1-Aug-2024 basic salaries are Rs. 38mn for the MD and Rs. 37mn for the JMD, with annual increments of 8% and perquisites of 60% of salary.
  • A profit-linked commission of up to 5% of profits as computed under Section 198 of the Companies Act.

The ratio of the MD's pay to the median employee rose from 161x in FY16 to 579x in FY25. It eased to 497x in FY26, when the MD's pay fell 15.5% while the median employee's pay fell 1.6%.

Against profit, the two MDs' pay has been far steadier, which we read as the profit-linked commission at work (Exhibit 121).

  • Vishal Baid. His pay rose 372% in FY26 on his elevation to executive director.
  • Total promoter-family pay in FY26 was Rs. 493mn (our calculation).
  • ESOPs. The 2020 scheme grants options at an exercise price of Rs. 100, against the Aug-2024 QIP price of Rs. 1,880. The pool is 1,000,000 shares.

We read the scheme as a retention tool for the professional layer, since the promoters are not grantees. It is a deep-discount scheme, and its cost will show up as a share-based payment charge.

Capital allocation

QIP-2021 was deployed as planned. It raised Rs. 4.0bn in Feb-2021 with general objects and was fully used by 31-Mar-2023, Rs. 3.1bn of it on capex.

QIP-2024 is running behind on capex. The Aug-2024 QIP raised Rs. 10.0bn at Rs. 1,880 a share. Its objects were capex for new plants, inorganic growth and general corporate purposes; the acquisition money went out first (Exhibit 124).

  • The capex money lagged. By 31-Mar-2026, Rs. 620mn had been spent on capex against Rs. 3.0bn scheduled for deployment by FY26.
  • Reason given. The company attributed the delay to moving the Jaipur SEZ project to the Medical Device Park on the Yamuna Expressway (YEIDA), and rescheduled the spend to FY27-28.
  • Idle money. Unutilized proceeds were Rs. 4.8bn at 31-Mar-2026 and Rs. 4.7bn at 30-Jun-2026.

The monitoring agency's 4QFY26 report added a sharper point:

  • It flagged that Rs. 719mn of unutilized proceeds had been placed in hybrid mutual funds with equity exposure (market value Rs. 759mn), and that Rs. 50mn was in a Nuvama absolute-return PMS.
  • It called the hybrid-fund placement "not in line with the disclosures made by the Company in the Placement Document".
  • The board's recorded comment was "No Comments".

We view the amount as small, but the episode is a data point on treasury discipline.

Acquisitions. The acquisition record is short and mixed. Plan1 has grown into a profitable if small business (Exhibit 126). PendraCare lost money in its first six months under Polymed. Citieffe was close to breakeven after closing, although its CY25 statutory accounts show a net loss driven by an FX loss. Integration of both is only beginning. Management has said it wants to "pause and absorb".

Its stated M&A criteria are unchanged:

  • technology and regulatory approvals in adjacencies chosen by disease burden: renal, cardiology, critical care, oncology and orthopaedics;
  • buying time, because organic development takes 3-5 years.

Cash and payout. Polymed ended FY26 with net cash of ~Rs. 5.0bn and liquidity of Rs. 8.0bn+.

  • Other income. Other income is a large part of profit: it was 28% of consolidated PBT in FY26 (our calculation), and its share of standalone PBT has risen (Exhibit 127).
  • Payout. The dividend was Rs. 3.5 a share, a payout of 11% of consolidated PAT (our calculation).
  • Management's rationale. In May-2025 it justified a lower payout by heavy capex and M&A opportunities.
  • Returns. Consolidated ROCE fell to 16.4% in FY26 from 23.5% on the company's adjusted definition, which already excludes unutilized QIP money from capital employed; we infer that the acquisitions entering the capital base, with their low early profits, explain most of the fall.

We see capital allocation, rather than the operating franchise, as the swing factor for returns through FY28:

  • the unspent plant capex at YEIDA, Palwal and Haridwar;
  • the turnaround of two loss-making acquisitions;
  • what happens to about Rs. 4.7bn of unused QIP money.

Audit, litigation and contingent items

Auditors.

  • Doogar & Associates audited Polymed until FY19. M.C. Bhandari & Co. took over from the 2019 AGM.
  • Doogar & Associates were re-appointed at the 29th AGM on 26-Sep-2024 for a term to 2029.
  • The placement document reports no auditor qualifications and no material frauds over the preceding five years.

Credit. CRISIL moved the outlook to Positive in Jun-2026.

  • Upgrade trigger: revenue above Rs. 22.0bn with an operating margin of 22-24%.
  • Downgrade trigger: a sustained margin below 20%, or larger-than-expected capex or acquisitions.

Total contingent liabilities listed in the FY26 annual report were Rs. 88mn (our calculation), against Rs. 535mn at 31-Mar-2024.

Most of the difference is the Rs. 424mn CGST show-cause notice. The disclosures do not say whether it was settled, dropped or reclassified, so we list it as a question for management.
