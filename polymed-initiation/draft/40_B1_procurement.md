# Part B – The industry {#part-b}

Part B describes the industry around Polymed: how disposables are bought and priced (B1), who it competes with (B2), the Indian market and its policy (B3), the regimes in its export markets (B4) and the structural trends that will reshape all of them (B5). Markets for individual product lines sit in the A4 chapters. Market sizes from commissioned industry reports and market-research firms are vendor estimates and are labelled as such.

## B1 How medical disposables are bought and priced {#b1}

A disposables maker does not set its price so much as meet the price of whoever buys: a US group purchasing contract, a European hospital tender, a distributor in an emerging market, a Chinese volume-based tender or one of India's three domestic channels. Each has a different decision-maker, contract term and price mechanism. That explains three things about Polymed:
1. why the same device sells at very different prices by region (Section A4.1 gives the MD's example of one BD product at {{CC-0451}} in Europe, {{CC-0452}} in the US and ${{CC-0453|plain}} in Africa); <!-- TR3Q23 p.15 -->
2. why a customer, once won, tends to stay;
3. why price policy reaches Polymed through some channels and not others.

### United States: group purchasing concentrates the buying

In the US, buying is concentrated in group purchasing organizations and hospital networks. A GPO aggregates the purchasing volume of its members and uses it "to negotiate discounts with manufacturers, distributors and other vendors". The GPOs' trade body counts {{WD-0004}} national, regional and local GPOs used by more than {{E-0379}}, and claims ${{E-0380|plain}}bn of savings a year, an advocacy figure. <!-- HSCA_HOME p.1 -->

The listed incumbents describe what this does to price:
- **BD** says GPOs and integrated health delivery networks "concentrate purchasing decisions", which "has led to downward pricing pressure", that consolidating health systems add buying power and that "traditional distributors are also manufacturers" and so compete with it. Price contributed {{E-0197}} to BD's FY25 revenue growth. <!-- BD_10K25 p.1 -->
- **ICU Medical** says large rivals win "full-line contracts" to supply all of a hospital's or GPO's infusion needs, and that a GPO contract "is no assurance that the sales volume ... will be maintained" because members may still buy elsewhere on price or quality. Most of its sales run under long-term contracts with prices "fixed for a set period of time", which is why it could not pass US tariffs on. One distributor accounted for {{E-0215}} of its revenue. <!-- ICUI_10K25 p.1 -->
- **Distribution** runs through acute-care, non-acute, laboratory and wholesale distributors; Cardinal Health and Medline warehouse and ship a large share of Baxter's products. Both also sell their own-brand products (Section B2). <!-- BD_10K25 p.1; BAX_10K25 p.1 -->

Polymed's route matches this structure. The MD described the market in 2023 as dominated by BD, B. Braun, Smiths Medical and Cardinal Health, with GPOs and distributors such as McKesson, Medline, PSS and Henry Schein, and noted that some GPOs serve large hospitals and others only day-surgery centres. Polymed has since signed contracts with two large GPOs and distributors, which "will be placing the product directly into the market", so that "our job is limited to manufacturing in India". <!-- TR4Q23 p.16; TR1Q25 p.4; TR1Q26 p.7 -->

We read the US as a market that pays the highest prices but rewards breadth of range and price stability over the life of a contract. Under fixed-price contracts a tariff lands on the supplier, not the hospital, which is why the tariff path in Section B4 matters more to the economics of a US contract than its headline price.

### Europe: public tenders, distributors and payback

European public hospitals buy through national and regional tenders. EU procurement allows a contract to be awarded on price alone or on the best price-quality ratio (the 'most economically advantageous tender', or MEAT, in EU usage), and the EU's own procurement instrument against Chinese bidders is designed around both kinds of award. Polymed's MD says European and US buyers give quality and performance {{CC-0454}} of the weight and price the remainder: "if you fail the first two criteria, price is never a criteria for them". <!-- TFX_10K25 p.1; EU_IPI2025_1197 pp.6-7; TR3Q23 pp.15-16 -->

Three features of European buying matter for Polymed:
- **The distributor is the customer.** Outside Italy, Polymed sells through distributors, which normally hold {{WD-0021|range}} months of inventory to keep supplying national systems; the FY26 destocking showed how quickly that buffer can be run down (Section A3). Management has added its own clinical team to support distributors at hospital level. <!-- TR1Q26 p.9; TR3Q26 p.12 -->
- **Approval comes before the bid.** The MD's summary: "CE mark doesn't mean anything unless and until you have approvals in local market ... and then you bid." In orthopaedics, Citieffe's management adds that many public tenders accept only bidders with a full portfolio. The UK's NHS delayed contracts by {{CA-0290|range}} years after the change of government. <!-- TR2Q26 pp.9-10; TRCIT p.10; TR3Q26 p.16 -->
- **Italy's payback.** Under a 2015 law, device suppliers to the Italian national health system must repay part of any spending above regional ceilings. The Constitutional Court upheld the law in Jul-2024, and a 2025 change let companies settle the 2015-2018 claims at about {{E-0207|plain}}% of the invoiced amounts. BD booked {{E-0206}} of payback accruals in FY24. Italian public spending on devices and services is about {{E-0384}}, per the industry association. This matters to Plan1Health and Citieffe, which sell into Italian public hospitals, and to Italy as Polymed's only direct European market. <!-- TFX_10K25 p.1; BD_10K25 p.1; CDM_NUMERI25 p.1; TR1Q27S p.5 -->

National and regional tenders, volume-based procurement and payback provisions all appear in Teleflex's list of pricing risks. From Jun-2025 the EU also excludes Chinese bidders from device tenders of {{WD-0022}} and above (Section B4). <!-- TFX_10K25 p.1; EU_IPI2025_1197 p.12 -->

### Emerging markets: the distributor holds the registration

In most of Latin America, Africa, West Asia and Asia a regional distributor or merchant exporter holds the product registration and the permission to sell. Replacing one is "operationally complex and time-consuming", which makes distributors sticky in both directions. Polymed sold through {{D-0007}} distributors outside India in 1QFY25, "with many of them benefiting from local/regional exclusivity arrangements". <!-- DRHP_GSURG pp.212-213; PD24 p.167 -->

Two markets show how the registration shapes the route to market:
- **Brazil** requires a Brazilian legal entity to hold the ANVISA registration. Polymed's distributors there "only sell our existing products"; for new ranges it will sell direct, and it bought Medyneo, a dormant company holding ANVISA and import licences, which management says saves {{CA-0143|range}} months. <!-- ANVISA_RDC751 p.1; TR1Q27S p.8; TR4Q26 p.8 -->
- **Saudi Arabia** requires a marketing authorization held through a licensed local authorized representative. <!-- SFDA_REQ1 pp.4-5 -->

Payment is the other constraint. Management linked its cash conversion cycle of about {{CA-0080|range}} days in Aug-2026 partly to hard currency that is "not available in time in many markets". <!-- TR1Q27S p.14 -->

### China: volume-based procurement sets the floor

China buys most hospital consumables through provincial alliances and national VBP rounds, which trade a guaranteed volume for a large price cut. China ran {{E-0285}} VBP projects for consumables in 2025, covering syringes, IV cannulas, vacuum blood-collection tubes and infusion sets among others. The first national round, for coronary stents, cut their price by {{F-0248}}, to about {{E-0174}}. An EU investigation found that {{WD-0019}} of Chinese public tenders for devices contain explicit or implicit bars on imported products. <!-- KDL_AR25 pp.17, 21; NHC_VBP_2011 p.1; DRHP_SMT p.178; EU_IPI2025_1197 p.6 -->

Polymed barely sells in China, so VBP matters to it only indirectly: the winners use the capacity VBP builds "to attack overseas markets", Polymed's markets among them (Section B2). <!-- SANXIN_AR25 p.34 -->

### India: three channels with three price mechanisms

India's disposables reach patients through three channels that price in different ways.

1. **The trade channel.** Distributors and dealers sell to private hospitals and nursing homes.
   - Polymed moved in 2022 from one distributor per state to several, some dedicated to a single hospital, with separate specialist distributors for dialysis chains, diagnostics and blood banks. <!-- TR3Q22 pp.10-11 -->
   - Prices differ by hospital, and distributors adjust them for payment terms: good hospitals pay in {{WD-0001}} months and poor payers in {{WD-0002}}. <!-- TR3Q22 p.10 -->
   - Orders arrive case by case, not under long-term contracts. <!-- DRHP_GSURG p.204 -->
2. **Corporate chains and organized providers.** Large chains negotiate centrally.
   - Polymed introduced a single corporate-hospital price list with volume discounts from 1-Apr-2022. <!-- TR3Q22 p.10 -->
   - It names Apollo, Max, Fortis, Manipal and Aster DM among its chain customers, and NephroPlus, DCDC and Apex among its dialysis chains. <!-- TR3Q23 p.20; TR1Q22 p.4 -->
   - The MD says Polymed is "present in almost every corporate hospital in the country", but it covered only about a third of India's hospitals in Feb-2025 and put its share of wallet in the hospitals it serves at {{CA-0384|range}}. <!-- TR1Q27S p.8; TR3Q25 p.13; TR2Q26 p.13 -->
   - Organized dialysis chains buy centrally to get "cost-effective dialysis equipment and consumables". <!-- RHP_NEPHRO p.282 -->
3. **Government.** Central and state tenders, state medical corporations and the Government e-Marketplace (GeM), where manufacturers usually bid through distributors.
   - Tenders are "subject to cancellations, re-bids and stringent qualification criteria". <!-- DRHP_GSURG pp.177, 216 -->
   - The MD says they are won mostly on the lowest price, with "no significant quality advantage", and that many state governments take more than a year to pay. <!-- TR2Q26 p.11; TR4Q25 p.13 -->
   - Public procurement reserves syringes, needles, cannulas and urine catheters for local suppliers (Section B3). <!-- PPP_MD_LC21B p.1 -->
   - Dialysis under the national programme (PMNDP) is bought as a service: the private operator supplies machines, dialysers and consumables. The initial contract runs five years, extendable by five, at a price set by competitive bidding with a {{WD-0020}} annual escalation. PM-JAY pays Rs. {{E-0052|num}} per session on average. <!-- RHP_NEPHRO pp.280, 283-284 -->

### Who pays the MRP, and why the trade margin matters

Every device sold in India carries a maximum retail price (MRP) printed by the manufacturer. The MRP matters less than it appears, because of who pays it:
- **Package rates.** Under insurance and PM-JAY, the MD explained in 2022, "the whole package rate comes and then MRP doesn't matter". The MRP applies when a patient pays cash or is billed item by item. He estimated that MRP-based billing had fallen from {{CC-0205}} of hospital payments to {{CC-0206}} over five years and expected it to fall further as insurance spread; by May-2026 he put insurance at {{WD-0030|range}} of hospital payments. <!-- TR3Q22 pp.16-17; TR4Q26 p.10 -->
- **The MRP is sticky.** Under Para 20 of DPCO 2013 the MRP of a non-scheduled device may rise by at most {{D-0185}} in any twelve months, so a brand launched at a low MRP cannot catch up with rivals that launched high. The MD's example was a competitor with twice Polymed's MRP, a level Polymed "cannot jump" to under the cap. <!-- PD24 p.41; TR3Q22 p.16 -->
- **The trade margin is the gap.** The gap between the landing price and the MRP is the trade margin. The drug-price regulator (NPPA) capped it at {{F-0069}} of the price to distributor for six devices in 2021. The MD argued then that such a cap "does not impact the manufacturers it is only the trade margins". <!-- PIB_TMR_2107 p.1; TR1Q22 pp.5-6 -->

The gap can be very wide. In a Sep-2026 survey by Maharashtra's drug regulator, a Polymed IV set bought by the trade for {{F-0083}} carried an MRP of {{F-0084}}, {{D-WD-001}} the purchase price (our calculation). That was the lowest MRP and the lowest mark-up of the three IV sets in the survey (Section B3). <!-- FPJ_MAHFDA_2609 p.1 -->

We draw three conclusions:
1. **Polymed's price sits near the trade's purchase price, not the MRP.** Its infusion price points of Rs. {{WM-0007|range}} per device are close to what the survey found hospitals paying, so most of the spread accrues to the channel. <!-- TR4Q26 p.18 -->
2. **A cap measured from the landing price would compress MRPs and channel margins first.** The second-round risk is that hospitals and distributors, having lost margin, push back on purchase prices; a low-MRP brand could also lose less of its appeal to the channel than a high-MRP one. We cannot size either effect, because Polymed does not disclose how much of its domestic revenue is billed to patients at MRP.
3. **The package-rate shift works the same way over time.** As more treatment is paid for as a package, hospitals gain from buying cheaper consumables, which favours makers with a cost edge.

### What keeps a customer, and what does not

For hospital consumables the key buying criteria are product quality, surgeon preference and cost, and the decision-makers are "healthcare professionals, payers and hospital administrators". <!-- DRHP_HEAL pp.123, 129 -->

Four things make a won account sticky:
1. **Contract terms.** GPO contracts are multi-year, PMNDP contracts run five years or more, and Indian hospital prices are "announced for one year". <!-- ICUI_10K25 p.1; RHP_NEPHRO p.283; TR2Q22 p.10 -->
2. **Registrations held by the channel.** A distributor that owns the registration is hard to replace, and so is the maker whose product it has registered. <!-- DRHP_GSURG p.213 -->
3. **Equipment-linked consumables.** Dialysis machines are placed against consumable contracts (Section A4.3).
4. **Training.** Nurses and clinicians are trained on a device. Polymed runs {{A-0010}} customer-engagement programmes a year with {{A-0009}} clinical experts, and says its Indian programmes reached {{A-0137}} healthcare professionals in FY26. Under India's code for marketing practices in medical devices (UCMPMD 2024), it gave free samples worth between {{A-1235}} and {{A-1237}} a month in FY25, against domestic sales of {{A-1233}}. <!-- AR26 pp.3, 19; UCMPMD25 p.1 -->

The evidence of stickiness is in Polymed's own record: management said {{CC-0361|range}} of export growth in 2QFY23 came from existing clients, and the top five customers were about {{CC-0455}} of revenue in Feb-2023. <!-- TR2Q23 p.7; TR3Q23 p.16 -->

Stickiness has limits. In commodity lines and public tenders price decides, a GPO contract does not guarantee volume, and Chinese makers offer prices well below Polymed's (Section B2). <!-- TR2Q26 p.11; ICUI_10K25 p.1 -->

We see pricing in disposables as a slow ratchet: small annual increases, passed on only partly and with a lag, and bounded by tenders, reimbursement and cheaper imports. For Polymed the direction of travel is set less by its list prices than by its channel mix. The more it sells to private chains and export distributors on negotiated terms, and the less through lowest-price tenders and MRP-led trade, the less exposed it is to India's price-policy debate (Section B3).
