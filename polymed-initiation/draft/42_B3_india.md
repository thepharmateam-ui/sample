## B3 India medtech: market, policy and demand {#b3}

India is Polymed's fastest-growing region and {{CB-0011}} of its FY26 revenue (Section A1). It is also where policy moves fastest, and in two directions at once. Industrial policy protects and subsidizes local makers through production incentives, local-content rules in public tenders and trade remedies. Price policy pushes the other way, through the cap on MRP increases, package rates for insured care and a live debate on capping trade margins. This section sets out the market, the policy on both sides and the demand drivers.

### The dialyser anti-dumping case

On 25-Jun-2026 the Directorate General of Trade Remedies (DGTR) opened an anti-dumping investigation into disposable haemodialysis dialysers from China and Malaysia, on Polymed's petition. The case, the policy measure most directly aimed at Polymed's own problem, is set out below; Section A4.3 covers its effect on the renal business. <!-- DGTR_DIAL_PCN p.1; DGTR_DIAL_INIT p.1 -->
- **Scope.** The period of investigation is calendar 2025, and the injury period also covers FY23-FY25. Certain Fresenius dialyser brands are excluded. <!-- DGTR_DIAL_INIT pp.1-3 -->
- **Standing.** Polymed told DGTR that it is "the major domestic producer"; the only other one is Nipro Medical India, a 100% export-oriented unit that makes dialysers almost entirely for export. DGTR accepted that Polymed constitutes the domestic industry. <!-- DGTR_DIAL_INIT p.3 -->
- **Injury claimed.** DGTR's initiation notice records prima facie evidence that dumped imports rose "significantly in absolute terms", undercut and depressed prices, and left the domestic industry with "financial losses, cash losses and negative return on capital employed" in the investigation period. Because Polymed alone constitutes the domestic industry in the case, we read this as a statement that its dialyser business made losses in the investigation period; the company does not report profitability by therapy line. Management says it claimed an injury margin of {{CA-0053}}. <!-- DGTR_DIAL_INIT p.4; TR1Q27S p.4 -->
- **Process.** Questionnaire responses were due by 9-Sep-2026, and {{F-0110}} parties have registered, including Weigao's blood-purification arm, Jiangxi Sanxin and a Malaysian exporter. No preliminary finding had been published by 24-Sep-2026. Management hopes for a decision "between now and end of the year". <!-- DGTR_DIAL_EXT p.1; DGTR_DIAL_IP pp.2-3; TR1Q27S p.10 -->

Management says most Chinese makers ship to India under the ASEAN free-trade agreement ([[EXREF:b3_duty]]), and in May-2023 the MD described Chinese product being relabelled in Malaysia or Thailand and sent on to India duty-free. ### Policy, part two: the price of a device and the trade-margin debate

Section B1 explains how the MRP and the trade margin work. Here we set out where the policy stands.

**Today's rules.** Non-scheduled devices, which include all of Polymed's consumables, face only the Para 20 limit: their MRP may rise by at most {{F-0067}} in twelve months. Device prices have been set directly three times: ceiling prices on coronary stents and knee implants in 2017, and trade-margin caps on six devices in 2021, which the government says save consumers {{F-0070|range}} a year. <!-- PIB_MDDRUG_2004 p.1; PIB_TMR_2107 p.1; PIB_PLI_2512 p.1 -->

**The Aug-Sep 2026 push.** Three steps in six weeks turned trade margins on consumables into a live issue:
1. **Parliament, 7-Aug-2026.** The standing committee on health recommended that "the gap should not be more than {{F-0073}} between landing price and MRP for medical devices and drugs", and called for centralized, tender-based procurement of high-end devices and consumables by private empanelled hospitals. <!-- PIB_PSC176_2608 p.1; MB_MRP_2609 p.1 -->
2. **Maharashtra, 15-16 Sep 2026.** The state drug regulator's price-monitoring unit surveyed inpatient consumables and found mark-ups of up to {{F-0081}}, including on a Polymed IV set, and its commissioner asked the Department of Pharmaceuticals and NPPA to cap trade margins, issue guidelines on permissible price differentials and bring the items under DPCO. The drug regulators of Punjab, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu sent similar inputs. <!-- FPJ_MAHFDA_2609 p.1; MB_MRP_2609 p.1 -->
3. **The Health Minister, 18-Sep-2026.** After the department met private hospitals and FICCI on 17-Sep, J.P. Nadda directed it and NPPA to review the trade-margin exercises of 2004, 2008 and 2019 and to consult hospitals and industry. No public deadline has been set. <!-- MB_MRP_2609 p.1; ANI_MRP_260919 p.1 -->

**What NPPA can do.** Trade press reports that NPPA has no blanket power to impose a single margin cap across non-scheduled devices; a broad cap would need an amendment to DPCO, or trade-margin rationalization category by category, the route taken for anti-cancer drugs in 2019 and for six devices in 2021. Non-scheduled products are {{F-0072}} of the pharmaceutical market by value. <!-- MB_MRP_2609 p.1; MB_20PCT_2608 p.1 -->

**Who is on which side.** The Association of Healthcare Providers, which represents {{F-0088}} private hospitals, argues that MRPs are "fixed upstream by manufacturers and distributors", that hospitals do not charge above MRP and that most treatment runs on insurance package rates. The domestic manufacturers' association (AiMeD) backs trade-margin caps based on ex-factory or landed import prices. <!-- MB_MRP_2609 p.1; BS_MAHFDA_260916 p.1 -->

We see three outcomes, in rising order of impact on Polymed:
1. **No action, or monitoring only.** The status quo, with the {{F-0067}} limit on annual MRP increases.
2. **Trade-margin caps on named inpatient consumables** (IV sets, syringes, catheters), as in 2021. MRPs of the affected products would fall towards a fixed mark-up over the price to distributor. On the 2021 precedent the cap applies to the channel, and the MD argued then that it "does not impact the manufacturers"; the risk to Polymed is a second-round push by hospitals and distributors for lower purchase prices.
3. **A general cap of {{F-0073}} over landing price** across devices, as the parliamentary committee proposed. That would require a DPCO amendment and would compress the channel's margin on every MRP-billed product.

In every case the exposure depends on how much of Polymed's domestic revenue is billed to patients at MRP rather than on package rates or institutional contracts, which the company does not disclose. We read the survey's single Polymed data point as a sign that it sets MRPs below rivals', not as proof. <!-- TR1Q22 pp.5-6; FPJ_MAHFDA_2609 p.1 -->

The demand story is well known; what matters for Polymed is how it is paid for. The shift from cash to insurance and government schemes moves hospitals from itemized MRP billing to fixed package rates, which rewards cheaper, reliable consumables; management says the shift, together with dearer imports, is pushing hospitals towards domestic products. Dialysis is the clearest case: the government pays a fixed rate per session, private operators run many public centres, and every session uses a dialyser, a bloodline set and a pair of fistula needles (Section A4.3). <!-- TR4Q26 p.10; RHP_NEPHRO pp.280, 283 -->

We read India as Polymed's most attractive market on volume and its most uncertain on price. The volume case rests on insurance, PM-JAY and PMNDP; the price risk rests on a trade-margin decision for which no deadline has been set. We would watch the Department of Pharmaceuticals' assessment, the DGTR's preliminary findings on dialysers and any NPPA notification on inpatient consumables.
